# Module 10: BA Perspectives

Business analysis does not occur in a vacuum — it is always practised within a context that shapes which tasks are emphasised, which stakeholders are engaged, and which deliverables carry the most value. BABOK V3 Chapter 11 introduces five Perspectives that describe how business analysis is adapted to meet the demands of five distinct initiative contexts: Agile, Business Intelligence, Information Technology, Business Architecture, and Business Process Management. Each perspective does not override the core knowledge areas; rather, it explains how those knowledge areas are weighted, sequenced, and expressed within that particular environment.

Each perspective section in BABOK V3 follows a consistent structure: it defines the change scope (breadth and depth), describes the business analysis role and position within that context, identifies the unique stakeholder roles, lists the BA outcomes (deliverables), describes methodologies and approaches, outlines underlying competencies specific to that domain, and maps how each of the six core knowledge areas is applied or modified.

**ECBA Exam Relevance.** BABOK V3 Chapter 11 is directly testable. Candidates must know the defining characteristics of each perspective, the unique BA roles and outcomes within each, and how the six knowledge areas are emphasised differently across contexts.

## Sub-module 10.1: The Agile Perspective

The Agile Perspective (BABOK V3 §11.1, lines 18569–19265) describes how business analysis is adapted within teams and organisations that have adopted an adaptive, iterative approach to delivery. BABOK V3 frames this perspective not as a set of ceremonies or tools, but as a mindset shift: from comprehensive documentation to working solutions, from contract negotiation to customer collaboration, from following a plan to responding to change. For the business analyst, agile is not simply a methodology to comply with — it is a worldview that redefines what good requirements work looks like and what constitutes a complete and valuable deliverable.

---

### 10.1.1 Agile Values and Principles for BA

**The agile perspective begins with a fundamental orientation: the business analyst's role is not to produce specifications but to ensure that the right value reaches the team at the right level of detail, at the right moment.** BABOK V3 §11.1 (lines 18572–18574) opens the Agile Perspective with a precise framing: *"Agile is about having a flexible mindset, embodied in a set of values and principles and exhibited by a variety of complementary practices. Agile initiatives involve constant change."*

#### The Defining Characteristic of Agile BA Work

BABOK V3 §11.1 (lines 18575–18578) specifies how this mindset translates to practice: *"Business analysts conduct analysis and deliver work products at the last responsible moment to continually allow flexibility for change; detailed analysis work is not done ahead of time, but just in time to be effectively utilized by the agile team."*

This *last responsible moment* principle distinguishes agile BA from predictive BA. In a predictive approach, the BA attempts to define all requirements before development begins. In agile, detailed analysis is deferred until the team is ready to act on it — no earlier. This eliminates the waste of specifying requirements that may change before they are implemented, and preserves the team's flexibility to incorporate new information as understanding develops.

BABOK V3 §11.1 (lines 18579–18585) describes the guiding questions that agile business analysis keeps in front of the team:

- *What need are we trying to satisfy?*
- *Is that need worth satisfying?*
- *Should we deliver something to satisfy that need?*
- *What is the right thing to do to deliver that need?*

These four questions are not sequential — they are recurring. The agile BA returns to them at the start of every iteration, every backlog refinement, and every feature discussion. They prevent the team from building solutions that are technically correct but strategically unnecessary.

#### Agile BA and the Requirements Process

Agile Business Analysis (Aguanno & Schibi, line 76054) characterises the agile requirements process as fundamentally different in its epistemology: *"the agile requirements process relies on observation and trial-and-error — and the process leads to a much more efficient form of requirements documentation."* Where waterfall BA seeks to establish facts before development begins through comprehensive documentation, agile BA treats requirements as hypotheses that are validated incrementally through working software and stakeholder feedback.

This does not mean agile BA is informal or undisciplined. It means the discipline is applied differently: more frequently, in smaller batches, with tighter feedback loops.

#### Breadth and Depth of Agile Initiatives

BABOK V3 §11.1.1 (lines 18636–18667) notes that agile approaches are used across a wide range of enterprise contexts — not only software development. Organisations have applied agile principles to process engineering, business improvement, and organisational transformation. A single agile initiative may be confined to a single department or may span multiple teams, divisions, and business units. The BA's planning approach must adapt to this scope variation.

| Knowledge Area | How Agile Values Apply |
|---|---|
| BA Planning and Monitoring | Planning deferred to last responsible moment; plans updated each iteration |
| Elicitation and Collaboration | Continuous, collaborative, iterative rather than front-loaded and formal |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managed through prioritised backlog; scope constantly evolving |
| Strategy Analysis | Reassessed each iteration to ensure solution continues to deliver value |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Just-in-time decomposition; user stories at lowest level of detail |
| Solution Evaluation | End-of-iteration reviews replace formal approval gates |

### 10.1.2 The BA Role in Agile Teams

**The BA in an agile team is an active participant in every phase of the iteration — not a specialist who hands off requirements and waits.** BABOK V3 §11.1 (lines 18586–18592) states: *"Business analysis work is performed continuously throughout an agile initiative and relies heavily on interpersonal skills such as communication, facilitation, coaching, and negotiation. Business analysts are active members of an agile team and often facilitate planning, analyzing, testing, and demonstrating activities."*

#### The Distributed Nature of BA in Agile

BABOK V3 §11.1.3 (lines 18799–18807) makes a structurally important observation about role distribution in agile teams: *"An agile team may have one or more team members with business analysis skills who may or may not have the job title of business analyst. This recognition of cross-skilled team members expands the practice of business analysis beyond that of a single specialist role."*

On agile teams, business analysis activities can be performed by:

- a business analyst working on the team,
- the customer representative or product owner, or
- distributing these activities throughout the team.

This distribution does not diminish the importance of BA work — it expands its reach. The BA becomes a facilitator and coach of analysis practices rather than the sole owner of requirements artefacts.

#### T-Shaped Skills: The Agile BA Competency Model

Essential Scrum (Rubin, lines 33350–33384) introduces the concept of T-shaped skills as the ideal competency structure for agile team members: *"T-shaped skills mean that a team member has deep skills in her preferred functional area, discipline, or specialty... [but] can also work outside of her core specialty area, doing some testing and some documentation."*

For the business analyst, T-shaped skills mean: deep expertise in requirements elicitation, analysis, and communication (the vertical bar of the T), combined with sufficient breadth to contribute to testing, prototyping, process modelling, and even facilitation of planning ceremonies (the horizontal bar). The agile BA who can only write requirements documents is a bottleneck; the agile BA with T-shaped skills is a force multiplier.

#### The Product Owner: Partner, Not Substitute

Essential Scrum (Rubin, lines 27271–27286) defines the Product Owner (PO) role precisely: *"The product owner is the empowered central point of product leadership. He is the single authority responsible for deciding which features and functionality to build and the order in which to build them."*

In Scrum, the Product Owner is accountable for what gets built and in what order. The business analyst is not the PO and should not be conflated with it. The BA's role is to support the PO with structured analysis, requirements elicitation from diverse stakeholders, and translation of business needs into forms the development team can act on. The PO sets priorities; the BA provides the analytical foundation on which good prioritisation decisions are made.

#### BA Outcomes in Agile

BABOK V3 §11.1.4 (lines 18810–18813) identifies the principal outcome of BA work in agile: *"Open communication and collaboration is one of the principal outcomes of successful business analysis in an agile project."* Documentation is a secondary outcome — a support mechanism, not the goal.

|---|---|

| Elicitation and Collaboration | Acting as stakeholder proxy; facilitating team-stakeholder dialogue |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Backlog grooming; ensuring stories are prioritised and ready |
| Strategy Analysis | Maintaining alignment between product vision and business goals |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Just-in-time story elaboration with development team |
| Solution Evaluation | Facilitating end-of-sprint demos; gathering and acting on feedback |

### 10.1.3 Agile Planning and Backlog Management

**Agile planning is not the absence of planning — it is the redistribution of planning effort from a single front-loaded event into continuous, lightweight cycles aligned with what the team is actually ready to build.** BABOK V3 §11.1.1 (lines 18621–18625) specifies the mechanism: *"Agile teams deliver small, incremental changes and commit to prioritized work items for only one iteration at a time. This allows the agile team to handle emerging changes for the upcoming iteration with minimal impact. An iteration is an agreed period of work time."*

#### The Role of the Backlog in Scope Management

BABOK V3 §11.1.1 (lines 18630–18631) defines how agile manages evolving scope: *"During agile initiatives, scope is constantly evolving. This is managed by the backlog list which is continually reviewed and re-prioritized."* The backlog is not a fixed requirements document — it is a living, ordered list of work items that is continuously refined as the team's understanding deepens and the business context changes.

BABOK V3 §11.1.5.1 (lines 19057–19068) describes how BA planning adapts: *"In agile approaches, detailed business analysis planning can be deferred until work on an activity is ready to begin rather than done upfront as in predictive projects. An initial plan for business analysis activities is developed at the beginning of the project. The plan then gets updated prior to the start of each cycle to account for change and to ensure that the plan is always up to date."*

#### Just-in-Time Refinement

Essential Scrum (Rubin, lines 30200–30207) articulates the timing discipline that governs agile planning: *"This should happen in a just-in-time fashion. If we refine too early, we might spend a good deal of time figuring out the details, only to end up never implementing the story. If we wait too long, we will impede the flow of PBIs into the sprint and slow the team down. We need to find the proper balance of just enough and just in time."*

This is the central tension of agile BA planning: refine too early and waste analysis effort on requirements that will change; refine too late and block the team. The BA's judgment about *when* to elaborate requirements is as important as the quality of the elaboration itself.

#### The Emergent Product Backlog

Essential Scrum (Rubin, lines 30218–30222) captures the structural reality of the agile backlog: *"The structure of the product backlog is therefore constantly emerging over time. As new items are added or existing items are refined, the product owner must rebalance and reprioritize the product backlog, taking the new information into account."*

The BA's role in backlog management (grooming) includes: creating and refining Product Backlog Items (PBIs), ensuring stories are adequately described and sized for near-term iteration, and supporting the PO in prioritisation decisions by providing analysis of value, risk, and dependency.

**BABOK Guide Techniques for Agile BA Planning** (§11.1.5.1, lines 19070–19080):
- Backlog Management, Prioritisation, Scope Modelling, User Stories, Workshops, Estimation
- Agile Extension: MoSCoW Prioritisation, Relative Estimation, Retrospective, Personas

| Knowledge Area | Planning Approach in Agile |
|---|---|
| BA Planning and Monitoring | Initial plan at project start; updated before each iteration |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Backlog as the authoritative requirements register |
| Strategy Analysis | Product roadmap maintained and adjusted at program level |
| Elicitation and Collaboration | Planning sessions are elicitation events; stakeholders co-prioritise |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Stories elaborated sprint-by-sprint, not upfront |
| Solution Evaluation | Iteration velocity and accepted story count as planning metrics |

### 10.1.4 Elicitation and Elaboration in Agile

**Agile elicitation is not a phase that precedes development — it is a continuous process that runs alongside development, producing just enough information for the work immediately ahead.** BABOK V3 §11.1.5.2 (line 19082) states: *"Progressive elicitation and elaboration occur throughout an agile initiative."*

#### The Pattern of Agile Elicitation

BABOK V3 §11.1.5.2 (lines 19082–19105) describes the typical agile elicitation pattern: an initial high-level elicitation establishes the vision and scope, followed by iteration-level elicitation that provides sufficient detail for each cycle's work. The intent is to *"generate just enough detail to ensure that the work at hand is performed correctly while aiming towards the goals."* Agile approaches explicitly minimise the time between elaborating a need and implementing it — the shorter this gap, the less likely the requirement will be invalidated by changing circumstances.

Strong emphasis is placed on *collaborative elicitation*: workshops with stakeholders, co-creation sessions, and face-to-face conversation are preferred over written specifications passed through formal channels.

#### The Epistemology of Agile Requirements

Agile Business Analysis (Aguanno & Schibi, lines 76053–76054) contrasts the waterfall and agile approaches to requirements discovery: *"In waterfall, the requirements process is primarily based on finding out what people believe to be facts before the development process begins, and then producing a series of long requirements documents. On the contrary, the agile requirements process relies on observation and trial-and-error."*

This distinction has profound implications for elicitation technique selection. Agile BAs favour observation (working with users in their context), prototyping (showing rather than describing), and iterative reviews (building shared understanding through feedback on working software) over interviews conducted to produce a requirements baseline document.

#### Acceptance Criteria as Elicitation Output

In agile contexts, the primary elicitation output for each story is a set of acceptance criteria — the conditions that must be satisfied for the team to consider the story done. BABOK V3 §11.1.5.5 (lines 19191–19195) notes that *"agile teams tend to use user stories at the lowest level of decomposition, usually supported by acceptance criteria which capture the analysis and design details regarding how the stories should behave when implemented."*

**BABOK Guide Techniques for Agile Elicitation** (§11.1.5.2, lines 19106–19124):
- Acceptance and Evaluation Criteria, Backlog Management, Brainstorming, Collaborative Games, Prototyping, Reviews, User Stories, Workshops
- Agile Extension: Behaviour Driven Development, Story Mapping, Storyboarding

| Knowledge Area | Elicitation Approach in Agile |
|---|---|
| Elicitation and Collaboration | Progressive elaboration; collaborative workshops; face-to-face preferred |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Acceptance criteria as the primary analysis output per story |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Stories traced to backlog items; prioritised for upcoming iteration |

### 10.1.5 Requirements Management in Agile

**In agile, requirements are not approved and baselined — they are continuously refined, re-prioritised, and validated against working software.** BABOK V3 §11.1.5.3 (lines 19125–19131) defines the agile requirements life cycle: *"As agile initiatives unfold, the scope is defined with increasing specificity. The expectation is that the needs will change and that the design will evolve over the course of the project. Prioritization of features based on value and development priority drives the work done in each cycle. Validation of the evolving solution with the stakeholders occurs at the end of every iteration in place of a formal requirements approval process."*

#### User Stories as Lightweight Requirements Containers

Agile Business Analysis (Aguanno & Schibi, lines 76560–76562) articulates the nature of the user story as a requirements artefact: *"User stories are not a full specification of the requirement, but a placeholder for conversation about the requirement when more information is needed. The user story will be fully specified and clarified upon being brought into the iteration and the development cycle."*

This is a fundamental shift from traditional requirements management. The story is not a complete specification — it is an intentionally lightweight container that triggers the right conversation at the right time. The specification emerges through dialogue between BA, development team, and stakeholders during iteration planning and development, not through a formal requirements review process.

#### Strategy Analysis in Agile: Continuous Reassessment

BABOK V3 §11.1.5.4 (lines 19157–19166) describes how strategy analysis is applied in agile: *"Agile approaches are often used when there is uncertainty about the needs, the solution, or the scope of change. Strategy analysis is a constant part of an agile initiative to ensure that the solution delivered continues to provide value to stakeholders. Agile team members use strategy analysis to help understand and define product vision, and develop and adjust the development roadmap, in addition to conducting ongoing assessments of related risks."*

For every iteration, the proposed solution is reassessed against the current business context to ensure it continues to meet business goals. The adaptive nature of agile means that adjusting to changes in organisational objectives is expected — not disruptive.

#### Requirements Analysis and Design: Just-in-Time Elaboration

BABOK V3 §11.1.5.5 (lines 19180–19195) specifies the timing and nature of requirements analysis in agile: *"Needs are progressively elaborated during an agile project. Analysis and design are performed on a just-in-time basis, either just before or during the iteration in which the solution component will be developed."* Analysis done just before the iteration enables estimation; analysis done during the iteration enables construction.

Models and analysis techniques are used informally and may not be maintained after they have served their purpose. This is not lack of rigour — it is appropriate calibration of artefact investment to value.

**BABOK Guide Techniques for Requirements Life Cycle Management in Agile** (§11.1.5.3, lines 19132–19141):
- Acceptance and Evaluation Criteria, Backlog Management, Collaborative Games, Prioritisation, Reviews, Workshops
- Agile Extension: Kano Analysis, MoSCoW Prioritisation, Story Decomposition, Story Mapping

|---|---|
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Backlog as dynamic register; end-of-iteration validation replaces formal approval |

## Sub-module 10.2: The Business Intelligence Perspective

The Business Intelligence Perspective (BABOK V3 §11.2, lines 19267–19988) highlights the unique characteristics of business analysis when practised in the context of transforming, integrating, and enhancing data. Where the IT Perspective focuses on systems that support business processes, the BI Perspective focuses on the information those systems generate — and on turning raw data into actionable insight that supports decision making at every level of the organisation. The BA in a BI initiative is primarily concerned with the *information* dimension of the enterprise, not the transactional systems that create it.

---

### 10.2.1 Introduction to the Business Intelligence Perspective

**The Business Intelligence Perspective is grounded in a specific value proposition: data has no inherent value until it is transformed into information that enables better decisions.** BABOK V3 §11.2 (lines 19271–19273) defines the focus directly: *"The focus of business intelligence is the transformation of data into value-added information: where to source it, how to integrate it, and how to enhance and deliver it as analytic insight to support business decision making."*

#### The Single Point of Truth

BABOK V3 §11.2.1 (lines 19280–19284) defines the central architectural objective of a BI system: *"A key objective of a business intelligence system is the consistent definition and usage of information throughout an organization by establishing a 'single point of truth' for diverse business data. A solution architecture that can integrate multiple data sources from within (and potentially from outside) the organization provides the foundation of a business intelligence solution."*

The 'single point of truth' concept is the defining design principle of enterprise BI. Without it, different parts of the organisation produce conflicting reports from the same underlying data — a situation that paralyses decision making and erodes trust in data assets. The BA in a BI initiative works to establish and enforce this consistency at the requirements and data definition level.

#### Decision Support Across Organisational Levels

BABOK V3 §11.2.1 (lines 19334–19340) specifies that BI initiatives address decision making across three organisational levels:

- **Executive level:** supports strategic decisions
- **Management level:** supports tactical decisions
- **Process level:** supports operational decisions

Where information needs are initially identified at one level, the BA investigates implications at the other levels to assess the full impact of the change.

#### Supply-Driven vs. Demand-Driven BI Approaches

BABOK V3 §11.2.3 (lines 19600–19618) identifies two fundamentally different strategic orientations for BI initiatives:

- **Supply-driven** (lines 19604–19611): *"assumes the view of 'for a given cost, what value can we deliver?'. This approach maps existing systems data to define what data is available."* The implementation strategy is to progressively include existing databases in the BI solution architecture, replace or repair existing outputs, and explore new insights from consolidated data.

- **Demand-driven** (lines 19612–19618): *"assumes the view of 'for a given value, what cost do we incur?'. This approach starts with identifying the information output needed to support business decisions, and then tracing that information back to the underlying data sources to determine feasibility and cost."* This approach allows for implementation strategies not constrained by existing database structures and supports early exploratory usage beyond existing reporting.

The choice of approach significantly shapes the BA's analytical work: supply-driven BI begins with data inventories and data mapping; demand-driven BI begins with decision modelling and information requirements.

|---|---|
| BA Planning and Monitoring | Scope modelling to distinguish infrastructure vs. enhancement initiatives |
| Elicitation and Collaboration | Eliciting decision support requirements at executive, management, and operational levels |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing evolving information requirements across functional areas |
| Strategy Analysis | Aligning BI solution to strategic decision-making needs |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Data models, decision models, transformation rules |
| Solution Evaluation | Assessing accuracy, timeliness, and relevance of information outputs |

### 10.2.2 BA Tasks in Business Intelligence Projects

**The BA in a BI initiative produces a specific set of analytical artefacts that span the full data pipeline — from source system definition to information delivery and decision support.** BABOK V3 §11.2.2 (lines 19461–19545) defines six major categories of BA outcomes in BI work.

#### BA Outcomes in BI Projects

*Business process coverage* (lines 19462–19465): defines the scope of the change with a high-level overview of the business decisions within the enterprise to be supported by the solution. It identifies how information output will be used and what value it will provide.

*Decision models* (lines 19466–19468): identify the information requirements of each business decision to be supported and specify the business rules logic of how individual information components contribute to the decision outcome.

*Source logical data model and data dictionary* (lines 19469–19487): the source logical data model provides a standard definition of the required data as held in each source system. The data dictionary provides a definition of each element and the business rules applied to it: business description, type, format and length, legal values, and inter-dependencies.

*Source data quality assessment* (lines 19488–19492): evaluates the completeness, validity, and reliability of data from source systems. It identifies where further verification and enhancement of source data is required to ensure consistent business definitions and rules apply across the enterprise-wide data asset.

*Target logical data model and data dictionary* (lines 19493–19497): the target logical data model presents an integrated, normalised view of the data structures required to support the business domain. The target data dictionary provides the standardised enterprise-wide definition of data elements and integrity rules.

*Transformation rules* (lines 19498–19501): map source and target data elements to specify requirements for the decoding/encoding of values and for data correction (error values) and enrichment (missing values) in the transformation process.

#### The Three Types of Analytics

BABOK V3 §11.2.3 (lines 19557–19579) defines three types of data analytics as *"incremental solutions, with increasing levels of systems complexity, cost, and value"*:

**Descriptive analytics** (lines 19560–19569): *"uses historical data to understand and analyze past business performance. Business information can be categorized and consolidated to best suit the stakeholder's view including executive management dashboards, middle level management key performance indicator (KPI) scorecards, and operational level management charts. No assumptions are made as to which situations are of interest to the stakeholders, what decisions need to be made, or what actions might be carried out."* The BA focus is on information and communication requirements for standard reporting, dashboards, ad hoc reporting, and query functionality.

**Predictive analytics** (lines 19570–19576): *"applies statistical analysis methods to historical data to identify patterns, and then uses that understanding of relationships and trends to make predictions about future events. The particular situations that are of interest to the stakeholders are specified, and their business rules are defined."* The BA focus is on information requirements for pattern recognition through data mining, predictive modelling, forecasting, and condition-driven alerts.

**Prescriptive analytics** (lines 19577–19579): *"expands on predictive analytics to identify decisions to be made and to initiate appropriate action to improve business performance."* Statistical optimisation and simulation techniques are used to recommend courses of action.

### 10.2.3 Stakeholder Roles in Business Intelligence Projects

**The BI initiative involves a distinct set of specialist stakeholder roles that differ substantially from those in general IT projects, reflecting the data-centric nature of the domain.** BABOK V3 §11.2.2 (lines 19438–19448) identifies four BA-adjacent roles in BI work.

#### The Four BA Roles in BI

A business analyst working on a BI initiative may serve in one or a combination of the following roles:

- **Business analyst** (line 19440–19441): *"who is competent in the definition of business requirements and the assessment of potential solutions"* — the primary liaison between business stakeholders and BI solution providers.

- **Business intelligence functional analyst** (lines 19442–19444): *"who has an understanding of data mining and predictive analytic techniques, as well as skills in developing visualizations"* — bridges the gap between raw analytical capabilities and business reporting needs.

- **Data analyst** (lines 19445–19446): *"who is experienced at defining source systems data to be used for the required analytical purposes"* — focuses on understanding what data exists, where it resides, and what it means in business terms.

- **Data modeller/architect** (lines 19447–19448): *"who is skilled in defining the source and target data structures in logical data models"* — designs the structural representation of the data that will flow through the BI pipeline.

#### Underlying Competencies for BI BAs

BABOK V3 §11.2.4 (lines 19650–19668) specifies the domain-specific competencies required in addition to general BA skills:

- Business data and functional usage, including terminology and rules
- Analysis of complex data structures and their translation into standardised format
- Business processes affected, including KPIs and metrics
- Decision modelling
- Data analysis techniques including basic statistics, data profiling, and pivoting
- Data warehouse and business intelligence concepts and architecture
- Logical and physical data models
- ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) best practices including historical data tracking and reference data management
- Business intelligence reporting tools

These competencies distinguish the BI BA from a general-purpose BA. The BI domain requires a comfort with data structures, data quality analysis, and information architecture that goes beyond typical requirements elicitation and process analysis skills.

| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing requirements across source systems, transformation, and delivery layers |
| Strategy Analysis | Aligning BI investment to strategic decision priorities |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Collaborating with data architects on models; with business on decision logic |
| Solution Evaluation | Validating BI outputs against business decision requirements |

## Sub-module 10.3: The Information Technology Perspective

The Information Technology Perspective (BABOK V3 §11.3, lines 19989–20766) describes business analysis as practised in the context of changes to information technology systems. This perspective explicitly applies to *non-agile* IT initiatives — agile IT initiatives are covered under the Agile Perspective. The IT Perspective spans the full range of technology change: from minor bug fixes to enterprise system re-engineering, from in-house custom development to commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) implementations.

---

### 10.3.1 Introduction to the IT Perspective

**The defining characteristic of the IT BA is the role of translator: converting business language into technical requirements and technical constraints into stakeholder impact.** BABOK V3 §11.3 (lines 20003–20005) states: *"A business analyst frequently plays the role of the translator who helps business and technology stakeholders understand each other's needs, constraints, and context."*

#### The Design vs. Requirements Distinction

BABOK V3 §11.3 (lines 20013–20020) addresses a terminological issue that creates confusion in IT contexts: *"In IT contexts, the term 'design' has traditionally been reserved for solution or technical design performed by developers, IT architects, or solution architects. All work done by IT business analysts is covered by the term 'requirements', including concepts such as the definition and design of business processes, user interfaces, reports or other elements of the solution relevant to stakeholders outside of the implementation team."*

Business analysts working in IT contexts may prefer the term 'solution requirements' rather than 'design' to maintain a clear separation of responsibility between BA work (what the solution must do, from the user and business perspective) and technical design work (how the solution will be built, from the implementation team's perspective).

#### Three Key Factors for IT BAs

BABOK V3 §11.3 (lines 20021–20027) identifies three factors that IT BAs must consider when scoping their analytical work:

- **Solution impact** (line 20023): *"the value and risk of the solution to the business"* — a system that, if it fails, disrupts critical operations demands more thorough analysis than a minor enhancement to a low-criticality tool.
- **Organizational maturity** (lines 20024–20025): *"the formality and flexibility of the organizational change processes"* — mature organisations with defined SDLC methodologies impose specific artefact requirements; less mature ones require the BA to establish appropriate structure.
- **Change scope** (lines 20026–20027): *"the breadth, depth, complexity, and context for the proposed change"* — the number of systems, users, and processes affected determines the scale and depth of analysis required.

#### Value of IT Solutions

BABOK V3 §11.3 (lines 20136–20139) summarises the value orientation of IT BA work: *"Information technology systems are implemented to increase organizational value, which includes any support capabilities and processes that use the system. Business analysts seek to align IT functionality to these processes and capabilities."*

### 10.3.2 BA Tasks in IT Projects

**IT projects are initiated for one of five reasons, each of which shapes the nature and scope of the BA's analysis work.** BABOK V3 §11.3.1 (lines 20029–20070) identifies the five change triggers for IT initiatives.

#### The Five IT Change Triggers

**Create a new organizational capability** (lines 20045–20048): *"can be executed to transform the organization. These types of IT initiatives may drive the creation of larger programs to address non-IT changes, but are centered on a technology that alters the business environment."* This is the highest-complexity trigger — the BA must define capabilities that do not yet exist.

**Achieve an organizational objective by enhancing an existing capability** (lines 20049–20053): *"is part of a change that meets a defined need. This may include changes to meet regulatory requirements or to enable business specific goals. These types of initiatives often modify an existing system but may also require implementation and integration of new systems."*

**Facilitate an operational improvement** (lines 20054–20058): *"is undertaken to improve organizational efficiency or reduce organizational risk. The change scope, organizational maturity, and solution impact dictate whether these changes will be managed as a project, part of a continuous improvement effort, or as an enhancement."*

**Maintain an existing information technology system** (lines 20059–20065): *"is undertaken to ensure smooth operation of an existing IT system... This may include technology driven changes such as a vendor discontinuing support of a technology, scheduled releases or upgrades to a purchased software package, or technical modifications required to support architecture strategy."*

**Repair a broken information technology system** (lines 20066–20070): *"is undertaken when an IT system that is not performing as expected is changed to correct the dysfunction. The urgency of the repair is generally based on the level of disruption caused. In some cases the scope of the repair effort is very large, so the repair is managed as a project."*

#### BA Outcomes in IT Projects

BABOK V3 §11.3.2 (lines 20277–20292) specifies the full range of IT BA deliverables:

- Defined, complete, testable, prioritised, and verified requirements
- Analysis of alternatives
- Business rules
- Gap analysis
- Functional decomposition
- Use cases and scenarios, and/or user stories as appropriate
- Interface analysis
- Prototypes
- Process analysis
- Process models
- State models
- Decision models
- Context models or scope models
- Data models

The change approach (waterfall, iterative, agile) has a direct impact on which of these deliverables are required and at what level of formality.

|---|---|

| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing requirements through SDLC milestones; tracing requirements to test cases |

### 10.3.3 Stakeholder Roles in IT Projects

**IT projects draw on a wider set of BA-adjacent roles than most other perspectives, reflecting the technical specialisation required at the interface between business requirements and technical implementation.** BABOK V3 §11.3.2 (lines 20223–20262) identifies seven backgrounds from which IT BA activities may be fulfilled.

#### The Seven IT BA Role Types

BABOK V3 §11.3.2 (lines 20232–20262) describes the personnel who may perform BA responsibilities in an IT initiative:

- **A business analyst who works specifically with the business users of an IT system** — the canonical BA role, focused on user requirements and solution fit.
- **An IT business analyst who is the designated liaison between the technical team and the business group** (lines 20235–20236) — a technically-oriented BA who understands both business needs and system architecture.
- **A subject matter expert (SME) experienced with the current software implementation** (lines 20237–20238) — deep knowledge of the existing system but potentially limited awareness of alternative solutions.
- **A software user experienced with the daily activity of how the software is used** (lines 20239–20240) — can focus on usability and workflow but may lack broader business or technical context.
- **A systems analyst who has experience within the business domain, but does not have experience with the specific application** (lines 20241–20242) — analytical capability without system-specific knowledge.
- **A business process owner who has depth of experience with business capabilities or processes, but may not have technical or IT experience** (lines 20243–20244) — deep business knowledge but potential gap in technical translation.
- **A COTS representative who will allow for customised implementations of a packaged solution** (lines 20260–20262) — vendor knowledge combined with configuration expertise; valuable when implementing packaged software.

The specific role assigned to BA responsibilities in an IT project depends on the type of change, the required level of experience and knowledge, and available staffing.

#### Solution Impact Factors

BABOK V3 §11.3.1 (lines 20112–20122) identifies the solution impact questions that determine the depth of IT BA analysis required:

- What happens to the business if this system shuts down?
- What happens if the system performance degrades?
- What business capabilities and processes depend on the IT system?
- Who contributes to those capabilities and processes?
- Who uses those capabilities and processes?

Higher solution impact mandates more thorough analysis, more formal deliverables, and tighter stakeholder engagement.

| Elicitation and Collaboration | Engaging business users, SMEs, technical teams, and COTS vendors |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing requirements through sign-off and change control |
| Strategy Analysis | Engaging application owners and business owners on strategic fit |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Collaborating with systems analysts and IT architects |
| Solution Evaluation | Coordinating UAT with process owners and business users |

## Sub-module 10.4: The Business Architecture Perspective

The Business Architecture Perspective (BABOK V3 §11.4, lines 20767–21631) describes business analysis practised at the enterprise level — concerned not with individual projects or systems, but with modelling the entire organisation to show how strategic concerns are met and to support ongoing business transformation. Business architecture operates at a fundamentally different altitude than project-level BA work: where a project BA asks "what does this initiative need to deliver?", the business architect asks "how does this initiative fit into the enterprise-wide picture of what we do, how we do it, and why?"

---

### 10.4.1 Introduction to Business Architecture

**Business architecture is distinguished from project-level business analysis by its scope: it models the entire enterprise, not a single change initiative.** BABOK V3 §11.4 (lines 20770–20772) defines the discipline: *"Business architecture models the enterprise in order to show how strategic concerns of key stakeholders are met and to support ongoing business transformation efforts."*

Architecture provides descriptions and views — referred to as blueprints — that create a common understanding of the organisation for the purpose of aligning strategic objectives with tactical demands.

#### The Four Architectural Principles

BABOK V3 §11.4 (lines 20779–20807) defines four fundamental architectural principles that govern all business architecture work:

**Scope** (lines 20780–20784): *"the scope of business architecture is the entire enterprise. It is not a single project, initiative, process, or piece of information. It puts projects, processes, and information into the larger business context to provide an understanding of interactions, integration opportunities, redundancies, and inconsistencies."*

**Separation of concerns** (lines 20785–20795): *"business architecture separates concerns within its context. It specifically separates what the business does from: the information the business uses, how the business is performed, who does it and where in the enterprise it is done, when it is done, why it is done, and how well it is done."* Once independent concerns are identified, they can be grouped in specific combinations — or mappings — to analyse targeted business issues.

**Scenario driven** (lines 20796–20800): *"there are many different questions that a business tries to answer to provide the blueprint for alignment. Each of these different questions or business scenarios requires a different set of blueprints containing a different set of information and relationships, with different types of outcomes and measures to determine success."*

**Knowledge based** (lines 20801–20807): *"while the primary goal of business architecture is to answer these business questions, a secondary but important goal is to collect and catalogue the different architectural components (what, how, who, why, etc.) and their relationships in a knowledge base so they can quickly and easily be used to help answer the next business question that comes up. The knowledge base is often managed in a formal architectural repository."*

#### The Value of Architecture

BABOK V3 §11.4.3 (lines 20873–20885) explains how architecture models provide value: they provide insights into important elements of the organisation and how they fit together, highlight critical components or capabilities, and — crucially — when change is being considered, *"the architecture provides details on the elements that are most relevant for the purposes of the change, allowing for prioritization and resource allocation."* Because an architectural model shows how parts are related, it can be used for impact analysis to identify what other elements of the business might be affected by a proposed change.

|---|---|

| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing architectural artefacts and reference models |

| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Producing enterprise blueprints; capability maps; value stream models |
| Solution Evaluation | Assessing strategic alignment of proposed changes |

### 10.4.3 Stakeholder Roles in Business Architecture

**Business architecture requires a specific combination of analytical depth and executive engagement that distinguishes it from both project-level BA and technical architecture work.** BABOK V3 §11.4.4 (lines 21198–21213) identifies ten underlying competencies required of BAs working in business architecture, beyond the standard BA competencies.

#### The Ten Business Architecture Competencies

BABOK V3 §11.4.4 (lines 21200–21213) states: *"In addition to the underlying competencies, business analysts working in the discipline of business architecture require:"*

1. *"a high tolerance for ambiguity and uncertainty"* — enterprise-level problems are rarely well-defined; the architect must operate productively without complete information.
2. *"the ability to put things into a broader context"* — seeing individual initiatives within the enterprise-wide picture.
3. *"the ability to transform requirements and context into a concept or design of a solution"* — moving from problem articulation to architectural response.
4. *"the ability to suppress unnecessary detail to provide higher level views"* — architectural thinking requires abstraction; the architect must resist the pull toward operational detail.
5. *"the ability to think in long time frames over multiple years"* — architecture addresses strategic horizons, not sprint cycles.
6. *"the ability to deliver tactical outcomes (short term), which simultaneously provide immediate value and contribute to achieving the business strategy (long term)"* — balancing immediate deliverables with long-term architectural coherence.
7. *"the ability to interact with people at the executive level"* — the primary consumers and sponsors of architecture work are senior executives.
8. *"the ability to consider multiple scenarios or outcomes"* — architecture is scenario-driven; the architect must model multiple futures simultaneously.
9. *"the ability to lead and direct change in organizations"* — business architecture is not just a descriptive activity; it guides transformation.
10. *"a great deal of political acumen"* — enterprise-level work crosses organisational boundaries and requires navigating competing stakeholder interests.

#### Breadth and Focus of Business Architecture Work

BABOK V3 §11.4.1 (lines 20823–20836) specifies that business architecture may be performed across the enterprise as a whole, across a single line of business, or across a single functional division. However, a broad scope is required to manage consistency and integration at the enterprise level. The perspective operates at the executive and management level — not at the operational process level; it assesses processes at the level of the value stream.

## Sub-module 10.5: The Business Process Management Perspective

The Business Process Management Perspective (BABOK V3 §11.5, lines 21632–22500) describes business analysis practised in the context of developing or improving business processes. Where the Business Architecture Perspective views the organisation at the enterprise level through multiple lenses (capabilities, value, information, organisation), the BPM Perspective views the organisation primarily through a process-centric lens — focusing on how work is performed, how value flows across functional boundaries, and how process performance can be continuously improved.

---

### 10.5.1 Introduction to Business Process Management

**BPM is defined in BABOK V3 not as a tool or methodology but as a management discipline — a way of governing and continuously improving how an organisation performs work.** BABOK V3 §11.5 (lines 21636–21641) provides the formal definition: *"Business Process Management (BPM) is a management discipline and a set of enabling technologies that: focuses on how the organization performs work to deliver value across multiple functional areas to customers and stakeholders, aims for a view of value delivery that spans the entire organization, and views the organization through a process-centric lens."*

A BPM initiative delivers value by implementing improvements to the way work is performed. BABOK V3 §11.5 (lines 21644–21647) notes that organisations holding a process-centric view treat BPM as an ongoing effort and an integral part of the ongoing management and operation of the organisation — not as a one-time project.

#### The BPM Lifecycle

BABOK V3 §11.5.1 (lines 21653–21690) defines the four phases of the BPM lifecycle:

**Designing** (lines 21654–21657): *"the identification of processes and definition of their current state (as-is) and determining how we get to the future state (to-be). The gap between these states may be used to specify stakeholders' expectations of how the business should be run."*

**Modelling** (lines 21658–21663): *"the graphical representation of the process that documents the process as well as comparing current state (as-is) and future state (to-be). This phase of the BPM life cycle provides input to requirements and solution design specification, as well as analyzing their potential value. Simulation may use quantitative data so that the potential value of variations on the process can be analyzed and compared."*

**Execution and Monitoring** (lines 21664–21668): *"provides the same type of input as modelling but in terms of the actual execution of processes. The data collected as a result of the actual business process flow is very reliable and objective which makes it a very strong asset in analyzing value and recommending alternatives for design improvement."*

**Optimizing** (lines 21669–21690): *"the act of ongoing repetition or iteration of the previous phases. The results of business process execution and monitoring are utilized to modify models and designs so that all inefficiencies are removed and more value is added. Optimization may be a source of requirements and solution design definitions that comes directly from stakeholders and the user community."*

#### BPM as Continuous Improvement

BABOK V3 §11.5.1 (lines 21714–21716) notes that *"business analysts involved with business process management are frequently engaged in continuous improvement activities as they are often the ones most familiar with BPM."* The BA is not only an analyst of processes but a champion of ongoing process improvement — bringing structured analysis methods to the continuous cycle of design, model, execute, and optimise.

|---|---|
| BA Planning and Monitoring | Planning for ongoing monitoring as part of BPM initiatives; progressive elaboration |
| Elicitation and Collaboration | Engaging process owners, participants, customers, and regulators |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing as-is and to-be requirements; change control for process redesign |
| Strategy Analysis | Linking BPM drivers to strategic objectives; business case for BPM initiatives |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Business process models, business rules, decision models, performance measures |
| Solution Evaluation | Process performance assessment; measuring improvement against targets |

### 10.5.2 BA Tasks in Business Process Management Projects

**BPM initiatives are triggered by a specific set of business pressures — and the BA's analytical work begins with correctly identifying which drivers are at play, since they determine the scope, urgency, and approach of the improvement effort.** BABOK V3 §11.5.1 (lines 21718–21754) identifies the goals and drivers of BPM initiatives.

#### The 15 BPM Drivers

BABOK V3 §11.5.1 (lines 21725–21754) lists the complete set of BPM drivers — the business needs that trigger a BPM initiative:

1. Cost reduction initiatives
2. Increase in quality
3. Increase in productivity
4. Emerging competition
5. Risk management
6. Compliance initiatives
7. Next generation process automation
8. Core system implementation
9. Innovation and growth
10. Post merger and acquisition rationalisation
11. Standardisation initiatives
12. Major transformation programs
13. Establishment of a BPM Centre of Excellence
14. Increased agility
15. Speed or faster processes

Understanding which driver(s) initiated the BPM effort shapes every subsequent analytical decision: a compliance-driven BPM initiative requires different stakeholder engagement, different success metrics, and different scope boundaries than an innovation-driven one.

#### The Four Process Improvement Approaches

BABOK V3 §11.5.1 (lines 21796–21810) categorises process improvement approaches along two dimensions — point of origin (senior management vs. front-line) and solution type (people-based vs. technology-based):

- **Top-down** (lines 21801–21804): *"initiatives are typically orchestrated from a central point of control by senior management and have organization spanning implications, targeted at end-to-end processes or major parts of the business."*
- **Bottom-up** (lines 21805–21807): *"initiatives are typically tactical approaches to improving individual processes and departmental workflows, or sub-processes in smaller parts of the organization."*
- **People-centric** (lines 21808–21809): *"initiatives where the principal change is to the activities and workflows in an organization."*
- **IT-centric** (line 21810): *"initiatives frequently focused on process automation."*

These categories frequently combine: a top-down, IT-centric initiative is a large-scale process automation programme; a bottom-up, people-centric initiative is a departmental workflow improvement effort.

#### BA Outcomes in BPM Projects

BABOK V3 §11.5.2 (lines 21951–21958) lists the five primary outcome categories for BPM BA work: *business process models, business rules, process performance measures, business decisions,* and *process performance assessment.*

**Business process models** (lines 21959–21970): start at the highest level as an end-to-end model of the whole process and can become as specific as individual workflow steps. Current state (as-is) models portray the process as it currently functions; future state (to-be) models envision what the process would look like with all improvement options incorporated; transition models describe interim states.

**Business rules** (lines 21971–21982): guide business processes and assert business structure or control behaviour. They are identified during requirements elicitation and process analysis, focusing on business calculations, access control issues, and policies.

**Process performance measures** (lines 21983–21992): parameters used to identify process improvement opportunities. They address quality, time, cost, agility, efficiency, effectiveness, responsiveness, adaptability, flexibility, customer satisfaction, velocity, variability, visibility, variety, rework, and volume.

**Business decisions**: identify the decision points within processes where rules and criteria determine process flow and outcomes.

**Process performance assessment**: evaluates whether implemented changes have achieved the performance improvements targeted by the BPM initiative.

| Elicitation and Collaboration | Eliciting as-is processes from participants; co-designing to-be with process owners |
| Requirements Life Cycle Management | Managing as-is/to-be process requirements; tracing changes to performance measures |
| Strategy Analysis | Linking BPM drivers to business strategy; defining performance objectives |
| Requirements Analysis and Design Definition | Process models, business rules, decision models, performance measures, solution specs |

### 10.5.3 Stakeholder Roles in Business Process Management Projects

**BPM initiatives involve a more complex stakeholder ecosystem than most other initiative types, because process change touches every person whose work the process governs.** BABOK V3 §11.5.2 (lines 21878–21925) identifies the three BA-adjacent roles within BPM and the broader stakeholder landscape.

#### The Three BPM BA Roles

BABOK V3 §11.5.2 (lines 21880–21925) defines three distinct BA roles within BPM:

**Process Architect** (lines 21881–21910): *"responsible for modelling, analyzing, deploying, monitoring, and continuously improving business processes. A process architect knows how to design business processes and how to enhance those processes either manually or for automated business process execution on a BPM platform."* Process architects address what process knowledge, methodology, and technology is required to meet the organisation's objectives. They are responsible for developing and maintaining standards and the repository of reference models for products and services, business processes, KPIs, and critical success factors (CSF).

**Process Analyst/Designer** (lines 21911–21919): *"has detailed process knowledge, skills, and interest. They are experts in documenting and understanding process design along with performance trends. Process analysts/designers have an interest in business process optimization to increase overall business performance."* They perform analysis and assessment of as-is processes, evaluate alternative process design options, and make recommendations for change based on various frameworks.

**Process Modeller** (lines 21920–21923): *"captures and documents business (both the as-is and to-be) processes. The process modeller is frequently a process analyst working to document a process for implementation or support by an information technology system."*

BABOK V3 §11.5.2 (lines 21924–21925) notes that *"the process analyst/designer and the process modeller functions frequently reside within a single position."*

#### The BPM Change Targets

BABOK V3 §11.5.2 (lines 21850–21876) identifies the primary stakeholders who are both sponsors of and targets for BPM change:

- **Customer**: the key stakeholder in any BPM initiative — BPM is customer-centric by nature.
- **Regulator**: stakeholders who may trigger BPM initiatives through compliance requirements.
- **Process Owner**: *"the key stakeholder in any BPM initiative and has the responsibility and authority to make the final decision regarding any changes to the affected processes"* — also responsible for measuring process performance.
- **Process Participants**: stakeholders who directly or indirectly participate in the process.
- **Project Manager**: manages the BPM initiative; works with process analysts, process owners, and process designers.
- **Implementation Team**: converts BPM plans into functioning business processes.

#### The Underlying Competency of the BPM BA

BABOK V3 §11.5.4 (lines 22219–22234) captures the defining disposition of a BA working in BPM: *"Business analysts working within the discipline of business process management are required to challenge the status quo, dig to understand the root causes of a problem, assess why things are being done in a particular way, and encourage subject matter experts (SMEs) to consider new ideas and approaches to make their processes more efficient and effective. They are also required to understand, articulate, and move back and forth between internal and external views of the processes under analysis."*

This combination — challenge, diagnosis, facilitation, and dual-perspective thinking — distinguishes the BPM BA from a process documenter. The BPM BA is not merely mapping how things are; they are actively working to understand why things are the way they are and how they should change.

BABOK V3 §11.5.4 (lines 22226–22234) adds: *"Business analysts frequently negotiate and arbitrate between individuals with different opinions, and expose and resolve conflicts between different groups within the organization. The business analyst is a neutral and independent facilitator of the change."* BPM initiatives are likely to involve all levels of the organisation, and the BA must communicate across organisational boundaries as well as with external parties.
