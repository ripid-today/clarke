# Module 1: Foundations of Business Analysis

## Sub-module 1.1: The BA Profession

---

### 1.1.1 What is Business Analysis?

**BABOK V3 Definition.** Business analysis is the practice of enabling change in an enterprise by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders (BABOK V3, Section 1.2). The word "enabling" is deliberate: the BA does not own the change itself but creates the conditions—clear needs, validated requirements, sound recommendations—that allow the change to succeed.

**PMI Definition.** Business analysis encompasses the value-oriented approaches, practices, disciplines, and mindsets that guide the identification of organizational needs; recommendation of potential solutions; elicitation, communication, and management of requirements; facilitation of successful solution implementation; and evaluation of the solution (PMI, Business Analysis for Practitioners, 2nd Edition). Tactically, PMI frames business analysis as the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to determine problems and opportunities, identify business needs, recommend viable solutions, elicit and manage requirements and other solution information, and define benefits and approaches for measuring and realizing value.

**Core Activities.** Both BABOK and PMI converge on six fundamental activities that define the discipline:

- **Identification** of business problems and opportunities
- **Elicitation** of needs and constraints from stakeholders
- **Analysis** of stakeholder needs to define requirements for a solution
- **Assessment and validation** of potential and actual solutions
- **Management** of the change, solution, or requirements scope
- **Evaluation** of current solutions and continuous process improvement

**Scope of Application.** Business analysis is performed on strategic, tactical, and operational initiatives. It can be performed within the boundaries of a project or throughout enterprise evolution and continuous improvement (BABOK V3, Section 1.2). Three of the six BABOK knowledge areas illustrate this scope explicitly: Strategy Analysis supports the pre-project rationale, Requirements Analysis and Design Definition supports delivery during the project, and Solution Evaluation supports post-project benefits realization (BABOK V3, Figure 1.1.1). This "beyond projects" framing is central to understanding BA as a continuous discipline rather than a phase-bound activity.

**Requirements Engineering as a Parallel Discipline.** Software Requirements Essentials uses the term "requirements engineering" to describe the same domain, broadly divided into requirements development (elicitation, analysis, specification, validation) and requirements management (change handling, version tracking, traceability). The four development subdomains are interwoven and repeated—not performed in a single linear pass—until a particular set of requirements is understood well enough for the development team to build and verify the solution with confidence (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1). This iterative nature is a defining characteristic of the discipline regardless of whether the team uses a predictive, adaptive, or hybrid approach.

**What Business Analysis Is Not.** PMI explicitly clarifies: business analysis is not testing alone, not project management alone, not data analytics alone, and not IT development alone (PMI, Table 1-1). Rather, it intersects with all of these disciplines while maintaining its own distinct focus on needs, requirements, value, and stakeholder alignment.

### 1.1.2 Who is a Business Analyst?

**Definition.** A business analyst is any person who performs business analysis tasks described in the BABOK Guide, no matter their job title or organizational role (BABOK V3, Section 1.3). Business analysts are responsible for discovering, synthesizing, and analyzing information from a variety of sources within an enterprise, including tools, processes, documentation, and stakeholders. The BA is responsible for eliciting the actual needs of stakeholders—which frequently involves investigating and clarifying their expressed desires—in order to determine underlying issues and causes.

**BA as a Role, Not Just a Title.** Software Requirements Essentials reinforces that "business analyst" refers to a role, not necessarily a job title: "Even if the team lacks an official BA, someone still must elicit, analyze, specify, validate, and manage its requirements. This work could be divided among multiple individuals, possibly including a project manager, quality assurance leader, and developers. When a team member who has another title is performing this kind of work, they are acting as a BA" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

**Common Alternative Job Titles.** Both BABOK and industry practice recognize that people performing business analysis work may hold titles including: business architect, business systems analyst, data analyst, enterprise analyst, management consultant, process analyst, product manager, product owner, requirements engineer, systems analyst, requirements analyst, and business systems analyst (BABOK V3, Section 1.3; Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

**Core Activities of the BA.** The activities that business analysts perform include: understanding enterprise problems and goals, analyzing needs and solutions, devising strategies, driving change, and facilitating stakeholder collaboration (BABOK V3, Section 1.3).

**Essential BA Traits and Skills.** A capable BA brings a particular set of knowledge, experience, personality characteristics, and skills to the process. Software Requirements Essentials identifies the following capabilities: listening, writing, interviewing and questioning, modeling, facilitation, flexibility across the abstraction scale, nonverbal communication, organizing information and activities, analytical thinking, handling interpersonal interactions, systems thinking, leadership, quick thinking, creativity, observation, and curiosity (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.2). Seven Steps to Mastering Business Analysis frames these through a "BA Suitability" lens, emphasizing that the most successful BAs naturally exhibit curiosity about how things work, a desire to solve problems, comfort with ambiguity, strong listening skills, and the ability to see both the forest and the trees (Carkenord, Ch. 1). For detailed coverage of competencies and behavioral characteristics, see Module 8.

**The PM–BA Dynamic.** Seven Steps provides critical clarity on the distinction between these two roles: "The PM manages project tasks (resources, timelines, tracking, and reporting) and the BA manages the solution (requirements, tracing, testing, validation, and operational support)" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). Project management and business analysis are two distinct professions with intersecting skill sets. Both require strong communication and collaboration, facilitation, and problem-solving. However, the PM is focused on scope, schedule, budget, and risk management, while the BA is focused on ensuring the solution delivers the intended value. Neither role has decision-making authority; both present clear definitions of situations with value-adding recommendations that lay a clear path forward.

**The Dynamic Duos Concept.** The quality of PM–BA pairing directly impacts project outcomes (Carkenord, Ch. 2):
- Strong PM + Weak BA: Requirements may be rushed, leading to rework late in the project when missing requirements are identified, typically resulting in schedule and budget overruns.
- Weak PM + Strong BA: Too much time may be spent in requirements gathering; the BA may allow scope creep if the PM does not enforce change control.
- Weak PM + Weak BA: The worst-case scenario—the project is likely to fail without strong leadership and clear requirements, regardless of how good the technical team is.
- Strong PM + Strong BA: The best-case scenario—a balance between thorough requirements gathering and project progress, producing high-quality outcomes on schedule.

### 1.1.3 The BABOK Guide Structure

**Overview.** The BABOK Guide is the globally recognized standard for the practice of business analysis. It describes business analysis knowledge areas, tasks, underlying competencies, techniques, and perspectives on how to approach business analysis (BABOK V3, Section 1.1). The Guide provides a common framework for all perspectives, describing business analysis tasks that are performed to properly analyze a change or evaluate the necessity for a change.

**Core Content: Six Knowledge Areas.** The core content of the BABOK Guide is composed of business analysis tasks organized into six knowledge areas. Knowledge areas are a collection of logically (but not sequentially) related tasks (BABOK V3, Section 1.4.2):

1. **Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring** — Tasks that business analysts perform to organize and coordinate the efforts of business analysts and stakeholders. These tasks produce outputs that are used as key inputs and guidelines for the other tasks throughout the BABOK Guide. (See Module 2.)
2. **Elicitation and Collaboration** — Tasks to prepare for and conduct elicitation activities, confirm results, communicate information, and manage ongoing stakeholder collaboration. (See Module 3.)
3. **Requirements Life Cycle Management** — Tasks to manage and maintain requirements and design information from inception to retirement, establishing meaningful relationships, and assessing and analyzing proposed changes. (See Module 4.)
4. **Strategy Analysis** — Tasks to collaborate with stakeholders to identify a need of strategic or tactical importance, enable the enterprise to address that need, and align the resulting strategy with higher- and lower-level strategies. (See Module 5.)
5. **Requirements Analysis and Design Definition (RADD)** — Tasks to structure and organize requirements, specify and model requirements and designs, validate and verify information, identify solution options, and estimate potential value. (See Module 6.)
6. **Solution Evaluation** — Tasks to assess the performance of and value delivered by a solution in use, and to recommend removal of barriers or constraints that prevent full value realization. (See Module 7.)

**Relationship Between Knowledge Areas.** The BABOK relationship diagram (Figure 1.4.1) shows that Business Analysis Planning and Monitoring provides guidance to all other areas. Strategy Analysis and RADD feed into each other as strategy-level needs are refined into detailed solution specifications. Elicitation and Collaboration and Requirements Life Cycle Management operate across all knowledge areas as supporting processes. Solution Evaluation provides feedback that may trigger new cycles of strategy or requirements work.

**Task Format.** Each task in the BABOK Guide is presented in a standardized format with eight components (BABOK V3, Section 1.4.3):
- **Purpose** — Short description of the reason for performing the task and the value created
- **Description** — Detailed explanation of what the task is, why it is performed, and what it should accomplish
- **Inputs** — Information consumed or transformed to produce an output; inputs need only be sufficiently complete to allow successive work to begin
- **Elements** — Key concepts needed to understand how to perform the task; not mandatory and usage depends on the BA approach
- **Guidelines and Tools** — Resources required to transform input into output; a guideline provides instructions, a tool is something used to undertake the task
- **Techniques** — Techniques that can be used to perform the task; the list is not exhaustive
- **Stakeholders** — Generic list of stakeholders likely to participate in or be affected by the task
- **Outputs** — Results produced by performing the task; may be a deliverable or part of a larger deliverable

Tasks are grouped into knowledge areas but can be performed in any order as long as the necessary inputs are present. The BABOK does not prescribe a process or a fixed sequence.

**Extended Content.** Beyond the core knowledge areas, the BABOK Guide includes:
- **Key Concepts (Ch. 2)** — BACCM, Key Terms, Requirements Classification, Stakeholders, Requirements and Designs (covered in this Module)
- **Underlying Competencies (Ch. 9)** — Behaviours, characteristics, knowledge, and personal qualities that support effective BA practice (see Module 8)
- **Techniques (Ch. 10)** — Common and widespread techniques practiced within the BA community; intended to be modified or extended by practitioners (see Module 9)
- **Perspectives (Ch. 11)** — Views of business analysis from different contexts: Agile, Business Intelligence, Information Technology, Business Architecture, and Business Process Management. Perspectives are not mutually exclusive. Each perspective has a standard structure: Change Scope, Business Analysis Scope, Methodologies/Approaches/Techniques, Underlying Competencies, and Impact on Knowledge Areas (see Module 10)

### 1.1.4 Career Paths in BA

**The BA Profession's Evolution.** Business analysis has evolved from a narrow IT-centric requirements role into a broad profession spanning all areas of organizations worldwide. Seven Steps frames this evolution: "BAs perform activities ranging from basic problem solving to complex systems thinking and strategic planning. The actual definition of the skill sets and techniques of analysis work is becoming more clear and easier to reference, yet the role of those who are performing business analysis continues to change" (Carkenord, Ch. 1).

**Common Entry Points.** Professionals typically enter business analysis through one of several paths:
- **IT to BA** — Developers, testers, or technical analysts who move toward the business side, leveraging their systems knowledge to bridge technical and business domains
- **Business to BA** — Subject matter experts, process owners, or operational staff who formalize their analytical skills and transition into dedicated BA roles
- **Project Management to BA** — PMs who discover they prefer requirements analysis over schedule and budget management
- **New to BA** — Recent graduates or career changers who enter the profession directly, often through certification programs or entry-level analyst positions

**Professional Certification.** Several organizations have developed bodies of knowledge and professional certifications for people working in BA and requirements engineering (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1):

- **IIBA (International Institute of Business Analysis)** — Offers three levels of certification:
  - ECBA (Entry Certificate in Business Analysis) — For those new to BA; tests foundational knowledge across the BACCM and all BABOK knowledge areas
  - CCBA (Certification of Capability in Business Analysis) — For practitioners with 2-3 years of experience
  - CBAP (Certified Business Analysis Professional) — For senior practitioners with 5+ years of experience
- **PMI (Project Management Institute)** — Offers the PMI-PBA (Professional in Business Analysis) certification, which integrates BA practices with project and portfolio management
- **IREB (International Requirements Engineering Board)** — Offers the CPRE (Certified Professional for Requirements Engineering) certification, focused specifically on requirements engineering practices

**Center of Excellence (COE) Structure.** As organizations mature in their BA practice, many establish a BA Center of Excellence—a centralized group that supports the BA community through shared standards, mentoring, training, and methodology governance. The COE helps ensure consistency across projects and provides career advancement paths within the profession.

**Career Progression.** Typical career progression follows a ladder from Junior/Associate BA through BA, Senior BA, Lead/Principal BA, and into management roles such as BA Manager, Director of Business Analysis, or Chief Business Analyst. Alternatively, BAs may specialize horizontally into enterprise analysis, business architecture, data analysis, or product management. Seven Steps emphasizes that regardless of title or trajectory, the core value proposition remains: "ensuring that the desired change produces value through defined designs and requirements" (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

## Sub-module 1.2: The Business Analysis Core Concept Model (BACCM)

---

### 1.2.1 The Six Core Concepts

**What is the BACCM?** The Business Analysis Core Concept Model (BACCM) is a conceptual framework for business analysis, established in BABOK V3 Chapter 2. It encompasses what business analysis is and what it means to those performing business analysis tasks regardless of perspective, industry, methodology, or level in the organization. It is composed of six terms that have a common meaning to all business analysts and helps them discuss both business analysis and its relationships with common terminology (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

**The Six Core Concepts.** Each core concept is an idea fundamental to the practice of business analysis. All six concepts are equal and necessary. Each core concept is defined by the other five core concepts and cannot be fully understood until all the concepts are understood. No single concept holds greater importance or significance over any other concept (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

1. **Change** — The act of transformation in response to a need. Change works to improve the performance of an enterprise. These improvements are deliberate and controlled through business analysis activities. Change is the central driver of all business analysis work—without a change to be enabled, there is no business analysis to perform.

2. **Need** — A problem or opportunity to be addressed. Needs can cause changes by motivating stakeholders to act. Changes can also cause needs by eroding or enhancing the value delivered by existing solutions. A need is not the same as a want; needs represent genuine gaps between the current state and a desired state that warrant investment to resolve.

3. **Solution** — A specific way of satisfying one or more needs in a context. A solution satisfies a need by resolving a problem faced by stakeholders or enabling stakeholders to take advantage of an opportunity. Solutions are not limited to technology; they may include processes, organizational changes, policies, training, or any combination thereof.

4. **Stakeholder** — A group or individual with a relationship to the change, the need, or the solution. Stakeholders are often defined in terms of interest in, impact on, and influence over the change. Stakeholders are grouped based on their relationship to the needs, changes, and solutions. Not all stakeholders have equal influence or interest, but all must be identified and understood.

5. **Value** — The worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder within a context. Value can be seen as potential or realized returns, gains, and improvements. It is also possible to have a decrease in value in the form of losses, risks, and costs. Value can be tangible (directly measurable, often with a significant monetary component) or intangible (measured indirectly, often with a significant motivational component such as reputation or morale). In many cases, value is assessed in relative terms: one solution option is more valuable than another from the perspective of a given set of stakeholders (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

6. **Context** — The circumstances that influence, are influenced by, and provide understanding of the change. Changes occur within a context. The context is everything relevant to the change that is within the environment. Context may include attitudes, behaviours, beliefs, competitors, culture, demographics, goals, governments, infrastructure, languages, losses, processes, products, projects, sales, seasons, terminology, technology, weather, and any other element meeting the definition (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

**Uses of the BACCM.** The BACCM can be used to: describe the profession and domain of business analysis; communicate about business analysis with a common terminology; evaluate the relationships of key concepts; perform better business analysis by holistically evaluating the relationships among the six concepts; and evaluate the impact of these concepts and relationships at any point during a work effort to establish both a foundation and a path forward (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

### 1.2.2 BACCM as a Mental Checklist

**Quality and Completeness Tool.** The core concepts can be used by business analysts to consider the quality and completeness of the work being done. Within each knowledge area description in the BABOK, there are examples of how the core concepts may be used and applied during the tasks within that knowledge area. While planning or performing a task or technique, business analysts can consider how each core concept is addressed (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

**The Six Diagnostic Questions.** The BACCM functions as a mental checklist through six questions that should be asked at every significant decision point in business analysis work:

1. **What are the kinds of changes we are doing?** — Ensures the BA has identified all dimensions of the transformation being pursued, including process changes, technology changes, organizational changes, and cultural changes.
2. **What are the needs we are trying to satisfy?** — Validates that the team is working on genuine problems or opportunities, not assumed or inherited solutions without clear justification.
3. **What are the solutions we are creating or changing?** — Confirms that the BA has a clear picture of what is being built, modified, or retired, and that the solution concept is well-defined.
4. **Who are the stakeholders involved?** — Ensures completeness of stakeholder identification—missing a key stakeholder is one of the most common causes of requirements gaps.
5. **What do stakeholders consider to be of value?** — Forces the BA to understand value from multiple perspectives, recognizing that different stakeholders may define value differently or even conflictingly.
6. **What are the contexts that we and the solution are in?** — Accounts for environmental factors that may constrain or enable the change, including regulatory, cultural, technical, and organizational contexts.

**When to Apply the Checklist.** The BACCM checklist should be applied at every major transition in the business analysis process: during initial scoping, when elicitation reveals unexpected information, when requirements change, when solution options are evaluated, and when solution performance is assessed. If any of the core concepts experience a change, it should cause the BA to re-evaluate all core concepts and their relationships to value delivery (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

**Practical Application.** Using the BACCM as a mental checklist prevents tunnel vision. A BA focused exclusively on functional requirements (Solution) may overlook whether the underlying Need has shifted, whether new Stakeholders have emerged, or whether the organizational Context has changed in ways that invalidate prior assumptions. The checklist forces a holistic view at regular intervals.

### 1.2.3 Relationships Between Concepts

**Mutual Definition.** The BACCM is structured so that each concept is defined by the other five. This means no concept can be fully understood in isolation (BABOK V3, Section 2.1). Understanding the relationships is as important as understanding the individual concepts.

**Key Relationship Pairs:**

- **Need ↔ Change** — Needs motivate changes; changes can create new needs. A business problem (Need) triggers a project (Change), but the act of implementing the project may reveal additional problems or create new opportunities that generate further needs.
- **Need ↔ Solution** — A solution exists to satisfy one or more needs. However, solutions can also create needs when they introduce side effects, require training, or change existing workflows.
- **Change ↔ Solution** — Change is achieved through solutions. The solution is the vehicle through which the desired transformation is realized. However, not all changes require new solutions—sometimes the change is to retire, modify, or replace an existing solution.
- **Stakeholder ↔ Value** — Value is always defined from a stakeholder's perspective. Different stakeholders may assign different or even contradictory values to the same solution. The BA must identify and negotiate these differences.
- **Stakeholder ↔ Need** — Stakeholders are the source of needs. However, stakeholders do not always articulate their needs accurately—they may express wants, assumptions, or pre-conceived solutions. The BA's role is to investigate and clarify expressed desires to determine underlying issues and causes (BABOK V3, Section 1.3).
- **Context ↔ All Concepts** — Context surrounds and influences every other concept. A change that is feasible in one organizational context may be impossible in another. A need that is critical in one market context may be irrelevant in another.

**The Hub-and-Spoke Visual.** The BACCM diagram (BABOK V3, Figure 2.1.1) positions Value at the center with the other five concepts arranged around it, illustrating that all business analysis work ultimately serves the delivery of value. Changes, Needs, Solutions, Stakeholders, and Contexts all connect to Value and to each other.

**Cascading Impact.** When one concept changes, all others should be re-evaluated. For example, if a key stakeholder leaves the project (Stakeholder change), their needs may no longer be represented (Need impact), the definition of value may shift (Value impact), and the solution requirements may need revision (Solution impact). This cascading nature is why the BACCM is used as a continuous monitoring tool throughout the BA lifecycle.

### 1.2.4 Applying BACCM to Scenarios

**Purpose.** The BACCM is most powerful when applied to real situations. This article demonstrates how the six core concepts work together in practical scenarios to ensure comprehensive analysis.

**Scenario Application Framework.** When approaching any business situation, structure the analysis by systematically addressing each BACCM concept:

1. **Identify the Context** — What is the organizational, technical, regulatory, and market environment? What constraints exist?
2. **Define the Need** — What specific problem or opportunity exists? Who identified it? What evidence supports it?
3. **Identify Stakeholders** — Who is affected by, interested in, or can influence the change? What are their roles and perspectives?
4. **Assess Value** — What does each stakeholder group consider valuable? Are there conflicting definitions of value? What are the tangible and intangible benefits?
5. **Describe the Change** — What transformation is being proposed? What is the current state and desired future state?
6. **Evaluate the Solution** — What specific solution components will satisfy the needs? Does the solution deliver value to the identified stakeholders within the given context?

**Common BACCM Pitfalls.** Using the BACCM framework helps avoid common analytical failures:

- **Solution-first thinking** — Jumping to a solution before fully understanding the need. The BACCM forces the BA to validate Need before evaluating Solution.
- **Single-stakeholder bias** — Optimizing for one stakeholder's needs while ignoring others. The BACCM requires identifying all stakeholder groups and their respective value definitions.
- **Context blindness** — Ignoring environmental constraints that will prevent the solution from succeeding. The BACCM treats Context as equal to all other concepts.
- **Static analysis** — Treating the analysis as a one-time activity rather than recognizing that all six concepts are dynamic and require ongoing monitoring.

**BACCM in Different Perspectives.** The framework applies regardless of the BA's working perspective. An agile BA may use the BACCM to evaluate whether a user story addresses the right need for the right stakeholders. A business architecture BA may use it to assess whether a capability model accounts for all relevant contexts. A business intelligence BA may use it to verify that a proposed analytics solution delivers value as defined by the data consumers. The BACCM is perspective-neutral by design.

## Sub-module 1.3: Business Analysis Principles

---

### 1.3.1 See the Whole

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in Systems Thinking, one of the underlying competencies in BABOK V3 Chapter 9. Systems thinking involves understanding how individual components interact within a larger system and recognizing that changes to one part of a system affect other parts (BABOK V3, Section 9.1). The principle directs the BA to step back from individual requirements or components and consider the full organizational ecosystem before diving into details.

**Application in Practice.** Seeing the whole means understanding how the initiative fits within the broader enterprise context: what other systems, processes, and stakeholders are connected to the change. It involves mapping the boundaries between what is inside and outside the scope, and understanding the upstream and downstream effects of any proposed change. As the BABOK states, business analysis can be used to understand the current state, define the future state, and determine the activities required to move from one to the other (BABOK V3, Section 1.2).

**Enterprise Boundaries.** For the purpose of business analysis, enterprise boundaries can be defined relative to the change and need not be constrained by the boundaries of a legal entity, organization, or organizational unit. An enterprise may include any number of business, government, or any other type of organization (BABOK V3, Section 2.2). This means the BA must define the relevant system boundaries for each initiative rather than assuming they align with the organizational chart.

**Practical Technique.** Software Requirements Essentials reinforces this principle through its emphasis on defining the solution's boundaries early (Practice #3). This involves identifying all external entities (users, other systems, hardware, regulatory bodies) that interact with the solution and understanding the context in which the solution will operate. The BA should create a context diagram or ecosystem map before drilling into detailed requirements.

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: consistently identifying cross-functional impacts of proposed changes; questioning assumptions about system boundaries; recognizing when a localized solution may create problems elsewhere; and proactively engaging stakeholders from adjacent business areas that may be affected.

---

### 1.3.2 Think as a Customer

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in the BACCM concepts of Stakeholder and Value. Business analysis requires understanding needs from the perspective of those who will use, be affected by, or derive value from the solution. The BABOK emphasizes that the BA is responsible for eliciting the actual needs of stakeholders, which frequently involves investigating and clarifying their expressed desires in order to determine underlying issues and causes (BABOK V3, Section 1.3).

**Users, Customers, and Stakeholders.** Software Requirements Essentials draws a practical distinction between stakeholders, customers, and user classes (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #4). Stakeholders are anyone who can influence or is affected by the project. Customers are those who commission or purchase the solution. Users are those who directly interact with it. The BA must understand all three perspectives but give particular weight to user needs because users are the ones who will work with the solution daily.

**Eliciting Actual Needs vs. Expressed Desires.** Stakeholders often present solutions rather than problems. Software Requirements Essentials describes this pattern: "A stakeholder might request a solution such as 'Combine several systems into one,' with the expectation that such a strategy would address multiple, unspecified objectives" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1). The BA's role is to investigate the "why" behind the request. Using techniques like root cause analysis and the 5 Whys, the BA can uncover the real needs driving the request—which may lead to a simpler, more targeted, or entirely different solution.

**Value Perspective.** Thinking as a customer means evaluating every requirement from the perspective of the value it delivers to the end user or customer. This does not mean the BA uncritically accepts every request; rather, it means the BA consistently validates that proposed solutions address genuine user needs. PMI reinforces this through its Business Value Assessment domain, where the first practice is "Understand the Situation" before proposing solutions (PMI, Business Analysis for Practitioners, Ch. 2).

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: routinely asking "Why is this needed?" and "Who benefits from this?"; conducting user research and observation before defining requirements; identifying user classes and characterizing their needs, frequency of use, and expertise levels; and challenging solutions that do not trace back to a validated user need.

---

### 1.3.3 Analyze to Determine What is Valuable

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in the BACCM concept of Value. Value is the worth, importance, or usefulness of something to a stakeholder within a context. The BA's analytical work must constantly assess whether requirements, solutions, and changes actually deliver value (BABOK V3, Section 2.1).

**Tangible vs. Intangible Value.** The BABOK distinguishes between tangible value (directly measurable, often monetary—such as revenue increase or cost reduction) and intangible value (measured indirectly, often motivational—such as employee morale, customer satisfaction, or brand reputation). Both forms of value must be identified and assessed. Intangible value is not less important than tangible value; it is simply harder to quantify.

**Value Assessment Techniques.** Multiple approaches exist for assessing value in business analysis:
- **Business objectives and success metrics** — Defining specific, measurable outcomes that indicate value has been realized (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #2)
- **Relative value comparison** — In many cases, value is assessed in relative terms: one solution option is more valuable than another from the perspective of a given set of stakeholders (BABOK V3, Section 2.1)
- **Potential vs. realized value** — Value can be potential (expected before implementation) or realized (actual after deployment). A requirement has potential value; the deployed solution has realized value.

**Value and Prioritization.** Analyzing value is directly linked to prioritization. Requirements that deliver higher value to more stakeholders in the current context should generally receive higher priority. Software Requirements Essentials advises: "When prioritizing requirements, favor those items that are the most critical or timely contributors to solving the highest-value problem" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1). Detailed prioritization techniques are covered in Module 4 (Sub-module 4.3).

**The Business Problem as Value Anchor.** Software Requirements Essentials defines a business problem as "any issue that prevents the business from achieving its goals or exploiting an opportunity" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1). The business problem serves as the anchor for all value assessment: if a requirement cannot be traced to the business problem or business objectives, its value should be questioned.

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: linking every requirement to business objectives or stakeholder needs; identifying and communicating the expected value of proposed solutions before implementation; challenging low-value requirements that consume disproportionate resources; and establishing measurable success criteria that can be used to verify value realization post-deployment.

### 1.3.4 Get Real Using Examples

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in the BABOK's emphasis on elicitation and validation through concrete examples. Requirements stated only in abstract terms are often ambiguous and subject to misinterpretation. Using real examples—scenarios, use cases, prototypes, and sample data—grounds the analysis in reality and exposes gaps, contradictions, and edge cases that abstract statements miss.

**Techniques for Getting Real.** Multiple BA techniques serve this principle:
- **Scenarios and Use Cases** — Walking through concrete sequences of actions that a user performs with the system, including both normal and exception flows (see Module 9, Sub-module 9.2.12)
- **Prototyping** — Creating visual representations or working models of the solution to validate understanding with stakeholders. Software Requirements Essentials devotes Practice #12 to creating and evaluating prototypes, describing the reasons to prototype, how to prototype, and the prototype's fate (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #12)
- **Examples in Requirements Statements** — Adding concrete examples alongside abstract requirement statements to ensure shared understanding. For example, instead of "The system shall support multiple currencies," provide "Example: A user in Vietnam entering a transaction in VND should see the equivalent in USD and EUR."
- **Data Examples** — Using sample data sets to validate data requirements, business rules, and calculations

**The Root Cause Analysis Example.** Software Requirements Essentials illustrates the power of examples through a detailed 5 Whys conversation between a BA and a stakeholder about combining systems. The concrete dialogue reveals that the stakeholder actually has two distinct problems (customer retention and support cost reduction), leading to a more targeted solution strategy (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1). This type of concrete, example-driven exploration is more effective than abstract questioning.

**Validation Through Examples.** Examples serve as de facto test cases. If stakeholders can review examples and confirm they correctly represent the desired behavior, the BA has validated the requirements. If examples reveal unexpected behavior, the requirements need revision. This "get real" approach pushes quality to the front of the development cycle, reducing costly rework later (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1, Recurrent Themes).

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: supplementing every abstract requirement with at least one concrete example; using prototypes and mockups to validate understanding before development begins; walking stakeholders through scenarios rather than presenting requirements in isolation; and creating sample data to test business rules and calculations.

### 1.3.5 Understand What is Doable

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in feasibility assessment and the BACCM concept of Context. Context includes technological capabilities, organizational capacity, regulatory constraints, and resource availability—all of which determine what is doable within the scope of an initiative. The BABOK positions context as equal to all other core concepts, meaning feasibility considerations are not secondary to needs or stakeholder desires.

**Dimensions of Feasibility.** Understanding what is doable requires assessment across multiple dimensions:
- **Technical Feasibility** — Can the proposed solution be built with available or obtainable technology? Does the team have the skills and infrastructure required?
- **Organizational Feasibility** — Can the organization absorb the change? Is there sufficient sponsorship, cultural readiness, and change management capacity?
- **Economic Feasibility** — Does the expected value justify the investment? Are the costs sustainable within the organization's budget and financial constraints?
- **Time Feasibility** — Can the solution be delivered within the required timeframe? Are there external deadlines (regulatory, market, contractual) that constrain the schedule?

**Constraints and Assumptions.** Software Requirements Essentials defines a constraint as "a restriction imposed on the requirements, design, or implementation activities" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.1). BAs must identify constraints early because they define the boundaries of what is possible. Assumptions are believed-to-be-true statements that have not been verified; they should be documented, validated, and monitored throughout the project because invalid assumptions can undermine feasibility assessments.

**Risk as a Feasibility Factor.** The BABOK defines risk as "the effect of uncertainty on the value of a change, a solution, or the enterprise" (BABOK V3, Section 2.2). BAs collaborate with other stakeholders to identify, assess, and prioritize risks, and to deal with those risks by altering likelihood, mitigating consequences, removing the source, avoiding the risk, sharing it, or accepting it. Risk assessment is a core component of understanding what is doable—high-risk approaches may be technically possible but impractical.

**Practical Application.** Software Requirements Essentials advises: "Use risk thinking to decide which requirements practices to employ, when to perform them, when to stop, and how much detail is necessary" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1, Recurrent Themes). This principle prevents over-engineering and gold-plating while ensuring critical requirements receive appropriate attention.

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: conducting feasibility assessments before committing to solution approaches; identifying and documenting constraints and assumptions early; escalating feasibility concerns to sponsors and PMs with clear analysis; and balancing stakeholder desires with organizational capacity and technical reality.

---

### 1.3.6 Stimulate Collaboration

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in the BABOK knowledge area of Elicitation and Collaboration and the BACCM concept of Stakeholder. Requirements engineering is fundamentally a collaborative process: "Requirements affect all stakeholders. Many people can supply input to the requirements, many people do work based on them, and many people use the resultant solution" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1, Recurrent Themes). The BA must actively stimulate collaboration among diverse stakeholders to produce comprehensive, accurate, and validated requirements. Detailed elicitation techniques are covered in Module 3; this article focuses on the underlying principle.

**Why Collaboration Matters.** Seven Steps emphasizes that "your communication, collaboration, and facilitation skills are often more valuable than your knowledge of analysis techniques" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). The BA's effectiveness depends not on technical analysis alone but on the ability to bring the right people together, create an environment where they share openly, and synthesize diverse inputs into a coherent set of requirements.

**Building Trust.** Trust is the foundation of effective collaboration. Seven Steps identifies trust-building as a core BA competency: "Successful BAs build numerous trusting relationships with people inside and outside of their organizations. These relationships help the BA to be successful not only on the current challenge, but also to have a solid foundation on which to be successful throughout many future endeavors" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). Trust is built through competence, reliability, empathy, and respect for stakeholders' time and expertise.

**Multiple Participants, Different Interests.** Software Requirements Essentials notes that "most requirements decisions involve multiple participants with different, and sometimes conflicting, interests and priorities" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1). The BA must facilitate negotiation and consensus-building among these diverse interests while keeping the initiative aligned with business objectives. This includes managing situations where stakeholders have unequal power or influence.

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: proactively engaging all relevant stakeholder groups, not just the most vocal or powerful; creating safe environments for stakeholders to share concerns and ideas; facilitating structured collaboration sessions that produce actionable outcomes; and resolving conflicts through analysis and negotiation rather than avoidance.

---

### 1.3.7 Avoid Waste

**Foundation.** This principle is grounded in efficiency and value-focused practices emphasized across multiple sources. The BABOK's iterative approach to business analysis tasks implies that work should be done at the right level of detail for the current decision point—not more, not less. Software Requirements Essentials frames this through the lens of minimizing rework: "A powerful way to increase development productivity is to minimize the amount of rework the team must perform. Therefore, try to push quality activities to the front of the development cycle—that is, earlier rather than later" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1, Recurrent Themes).

**Sources of Waste in Business Analysis.** Common forms of waste that BAs should guard against:
- **Gold-plating** — Adding features or requirements that no stakeholder requested and that do not trace to business objectives
- **Over-documentation** — Producing requirements artifacts at a level of detail that exceeds what is needed for the current audience and decision point
- **Under-documentation** — Producing insufficient documentation that leads to miscommunication, rework, and defects later
- **Solving the wrong problem** — Software Requirements Essentials warns: "Far too often, teams build and release requirements, features, and even entire products that go unused because those teams didn't fully understand the business situation and the problems they were trying to solve" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1). This is the most costly form of waste—project success but product failure.
- **Redundant elicitation** — Gathering the same information from multiple sources without consolidation, or failing to reuse existing requirements-related items from other projects

**Right-Sizing Requirements Work.** Software Requirements Essentials advises that the level of requirements detail should be calibrated by risk: "The risks of miscommunication and wasted effort are greater when development is outsourced or teams are remote than when participants work in proximity. Therefore, requirements for such projects must be written more precisely and in more detail" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1). This means there is no single "correct" level of documentation—the BA must assess the context and tailor the effort accordingly.

**Reuse as Waste Prevention.** The BA should look for existing requirements-related items from other projects that can be reused. Glossaries, business rules, process descriptions, stakeholder catalogs, data models, security requirements, and the like can apply to multiple situations. Once an organization invests in creating these artifacts, it should organize them to enable reuse and look for opportunities to leverage that investment further (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

**Effectiveness Indicators.** A BA demonstrates this principle by: calibrating documentation detail to project risk and audience needs; identifying opportunities to reuse existing artifacts; questioning whether proposed features trace to validated business needs; and investing early in quality (reviews, prototypes, validation) to prevent costly downstream rework.

## Sub-module 1.4: Key Terms & Requirements Classification

---

### 1.4.1 Core BA Vocabulary

**Purpose.** A shared vocabulary is essential for effective business analysis communication. The BABOK V3 Chapter 2 defines key terms that provide the foundation for all content in the Guide. Software Requirements Essentials warns that "a software team must deal with many types of requirements-related knowledge, and people will be confused if they lack a common understanding of them" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

**BABOK V3 Key Terms (Section 2.2):**

- **Business Analysis** — The practice of enabling change in an enterprise by defining needs and recommending solutions that deliver value to stakeholders.
- **Business Analysis Information** — The broad and diverse sets of information that business analysts analyze, transform, and report. It is information of any kind—at any level of detail—that is used as an input to, or is an output of, business analysis work. Examples include elicitation results, requirements, designs, solution options, solution scope, and change strategy. The BABOK deliberately uses "information" rather than "requirements" to ensure all inputs and outputs are subject to BA tasks and activities.
- **Design** — A usable representation of a solution. Design focuses on understanding how value might be realized by a solution if it is built. The nature of the representation may be a document (or set of documents) and can vary widely depending on circumstances.
- **Enterprise** — A system of one or more organizations and the solutions they use to pursue a shared set of common goals. These solutions (also referred to as organizational capabilities) can be processes, tools, or information. For the purpose of business analysis, enterprise boundaries can be defined relative to the change and need not be constrained by legal entity boundaries.
- **Organization** — An autonomous group of people under the management of a single individual or board, working toward common goals and objectives. Organizations often have clearly defined boundaries and operate on a continuous basis, as opposed to project teams, which may be disbanded once objectives are achieved.
- **Plan** — A proposal for doing or achieving something. Plans describe a set of events, the dependencies among the events, the expected sequence, the schedule, the results or outcomes, the materials and resources needed, and the stakeholders involved.
- **Requirement** — A usable representation of a need. Requirements focus on understanding what kind of value could be delivered if a requirement is fulfilled. The nature of the representation may be a document (or set of documents) but can vary widely.
- **Risk** — The effect of uncertainty on the value of a change, a solution, or the enterprise.

**Software Requirements Essentials Additional Terms (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.1):**

- **Business Rule** — A directive that defines or restricts actions within an organization's operations. A policy, regulation, law, or standard that leads to derived solution requirements that enforce or comply with it.
- **Constraint** — A restriction imposed on the requirements, design, or implementation activities.
- **Data Requirement** — A definition of a data object or element that the system must manipulate, its composition and attributes, relationships among data objects, and input/output formats.
- **External Interface Requirement** — A description of a connection between the solution being built and other elements of the world around it, including users, other software systems, hardware devices, and networks.

**Seven Steps Additional Terms (Carkenord, Ch. 1):**

- **Project** — An initiative launched to create a solution for one or more business problems or to exploit a business opportunity.
- **Product** — The deliverable of a project; may include software systems, manual operations, and business processes.
- **Solution** — One or more products, components of products, or enhancements or corrections to a product. PMI defines a solution as something that is produced to deliver measurable business value to meet the business need and expectations of stakeholders. Solutions may include technology, processes, people, and anything else included in the value delivered (PMI, Ch. 1).
- **Deliverable** — A tangible or intangible output produced as a result of project work. Not all deliverables are requirements documents; deliverables include models, prototypes, test plans, and trained users.

**The Glossary Imperative.** Software Requirements Essentials devotes an entire practice (Practice #17) to creating a glossary, emphasizing the importance of synchronizing communication across all stakeholders. Terms that seem obvious may carry different meanings in different departments. The BA should establish and maintain a shared glossary from the earliest stages of any initiative.

### 1.4.2 Business Requirements

**Definition.** Business requirements are statements of goals, objectives, and outcomes that describe why a change has been initiated. They can apply to the whole of an enterprise, a business area, or a specific initiative (BABOK V3, Section 2.3).

**Characteristics.** Business requirements exist at the highest level of the requirements hierarchy. They provide the guiding directives for all subsequent analysis and development work. If a project or product fails to satisfy its business requirements, someone wasted time, money, and effort (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #2).

**Components of Business Requirements.** Business requirements encompass several kinds of information (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #2):
- **Business problems or opportunities** — The situation that motivates the initiative
- **Business objectives** — Specific, measurable outcomes that the initiative must achieve to be considered successful
- **Success metrics** — Quantifiable indicators that will demonstrate whether objectives have been met
- **Product vision** — A concise statement of the desired future state
- **Scope and limitations** — High-level boundaries defining what is included and excluded from the initiative

**Business Objectives.** Business objectives let you determine when a business problem is solved, a need is fulfilled, or an opportunity is exploited. They can be financial or nonfinancial, internal or outward-facing, strategic or tactical. Software Requirements Essentials advises writing objectives to be specific, realistic, and measurable, and recommends stating absolute goals rather than percentage changes relative to a current starting point (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #2).

**Relationship to Other Requirements Types.** Business requirements sit at the top of the requirements model. They drive stakeholder requirements (what stakeholders need to do), which in turn drive solution requirements (what the solution must do), which may generate transition requirements (what must happen during the change). Developers do not directly implement business requirements; they implement functional requirements derived from them. The goal is to implement the right set of functionality that lets users perform their tasks, thereby achieving the business requirements within all imposed constraints (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

---

### 1.4.3 Stakeholder Requirements

**Definition.** Stakeholder requirements describe the needs of stakeholders that must be met in order to achieve the business requirements. They may serve as a bridge between business and solution requirements (BABOK V3, Section 2.3).

**Nature and Scope.** Stakeholder requirements describe what stakeholders need to do with the solution—the tasks, goals, and expectations that users and other affected parties have. Software Requirements Essentials uses the term "user requirements" with a specific focus: "A description of a task or goal that a user wishes to accomplish with the solution" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.1). IIBA generalizes this to "stakeholder requirements" because, in actuality, all requirements originate from some stakeholder. The broader term acknowledges that non-user stakeholders (regulators, operational support, sponsors) also have requirements that must be satisfied.

**Eliciting Stakeholder Requirements.** Stakeholder requirements emerge through elicitation techniques such as interviews, workshops, observation, and document analysis. The challenge is that stakeholders often present solutions rather than needs. Software Requirements Essentials illustrates this through the "Combine several systems into one" example, where the BA must dig through the presented solution to discover the actual stakeholder needs: higher customer retention and reduced support costs (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #1).

**Usage-Centric Framing.** Software Requirements Essentials emphasizes that stakeholder requirements should be framed in terms of usage—what the user needs to do with the solution—rather than in terms of system features. This user-centric perspective ensures that the solution serves real work processes, not hypothetical features (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #6). Use cases, user stories, and scenarios are common formats for capturing stakeholder requirements. Detailed elicitation techniques are covered in Module 3 and Module 9.

---

### 1.4.4 Solution Requirements

**Definition.** Solution requirements describe the capabilities and qualities of a solution that meets the stakeholder requirements. They provide the appropriate level of detail to allow for the development and implementation of the solution (BABOK V3, Section 2.3).

**Two Sub-Categories.** Solution requirements are divided into:

- **Functional Requirements** — Describe the capabilities that a solution must have in terms of the behaviour and information that the solution will manage. Software Requirements Essentials defines a functional requirement as "a description of some behavior that the product will exhibit under specified circumstances" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.1). Functional requirements specify what the system does.
- **Non-Functional Requirements (Quality of Service Requirements)** — Do not relate directly to the behaviour or functionality of the solution, but rather describe conditions under which a solution must remain effective or qualities that a solution must have (BABOK V3, Section 2.3). These include performance, security, usability, reliability, scalability, and other quality attributes. Software Requirements Essentials notes that "quality attributes describe various quality, service, or performance characteristics of the solution" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Table 1.1).

**Additional Solution Requirement Types.** Software Requirements Essentials identifies additional types that fall under the solution requirements umbrella:
- **Data Requirements** — Definitions of data objects, their attributes, relationships, and input/output formats
- **External Interface Requirements** — Descriptions of connections between the solution and external entities (users, other systems, hardware devices, networks)
- **System Requirements** — For complex systems with multiple subsystems (often including both hardware and software), system requirements serve as the origin of derived software solution requirements

**Derivation.** Solution requirements are derived from stakeholder requirements. The BA analyzes what stakeholders need to do and determines what capabilities the solution must have to support those activities. This derivation process is iterative: detailed solution requirements may reveal additional stakeholder requirements that were not initially apparent. Requirements analysis techniques for specifying solution requirements are covered in Module 6.

---

### 1.4.5 Transition Requirements

**Definition.** Transition requirements describe the capabilities that the solution must have and the conditions the solution must meet to facilitate transition from the current state to the future state, but which are not needed once the change is complete (BABOK V3, Section 2.3).

**Temporary Nature.** Transition requirements are differentiated from other requirements types because they are of a temporary nature. They exist only during the period of transition and are retired once the new solution is operational and the old solution (if any) is decommissioned.

**Common Transition Requirement Areas.** Transition requirements typically address:
- **Data conversion and migration** — Moving data from legacy systems to the new solution, including data cleansing, transformation, and validation
- **Training** — Preparing users and operational staff to work with the new solution
- **Business continuity** — Ensuring that critical business operations continue during the transition period, including parallel operation of old and new systems
- **Rollback capability** — Defining conditions and procedures for reverting to the previous state if the transition encounters critical problems
- **Interim processes** — Temporary manual processes or workarounds needed during the transition period

**Importance in Practice.** Transition requirements are often overlooked during requirements elicitation because stakeholders focus on the future state (what the new solution will do) rather than the path to get there. However, failed transitions can derail otherwise well-designed solutions. The BA must proactively identify transition requirements by asking: "What must happen to move from where we are today to where we want to be?" Transition planning within Strategy Analysis is covered in Module 5 (Sub-module 5.4).

### 1.4.6 Requirements vs Designs

**The Core Distinction.** Requirements are focused on the need; designs are focused on the solution. Requirements describe what kind of value could be delivered if a requirement is fulfilled. Designs describe how value might be realized by a solution if it is built (BABOK V3, Section 2.5).

**The Blurred Boundary.** The distinction between requirements and designs is not always clear. The BABOK acknowledges: "The same techniques are used to elicit, model, and analyze both. A requirement leads to a design which in turn may drive the discovery and analysis of more requirements. The shift in focus is often subtle" (BABOK V3, Section 2.5). This means the BA should not spend excessive effort classifying individual statements as "requirement" or "design" but should instead focus on ensuring that the need is understood and the solution is well-defined.

**Requirement-to-Design Examples.** The BABOK provides concrete examples of the relationship (BABOK V3, Table 2.5.1):

| Requirement (Need-Focused) | Design (Solution-Focused) |
|---|---|
| View six months of sales data across multiple organizational units in a single view | A sketch of a dashboard |
| Reduce amount of time required to pick and pack a customer order | Process model |
| Record and access a medical patient's history | Screen mock-up showing specific data fields |
| Develop business strategy, goals, and objectives for a new business | Business Capability Model |
| Provide information in English and French | Prototype with text displayed in English and French |

**The Recursive Cycle.** Business analysis can be complex and recursive. A requirement (or set of requirements) may be used to define a design. That design may then be used to elicit additional requirements that are used to define more detailed designs. The business analyst may hand off requirements and designs to other stakeholders who may further elaborate on the designs (BABOK V3, Section 2.5). The BABOK's Requirements and Design Cycle (Figure 2.5.1) illustrates this: starting from Business Requirements ("Why do I want it?"), through Stakeholder Requirements ("What are the needs?"), to Solution Requirements ("What do I want?"), to Transition Requirements ("What are the conditions?"), with design work occurring at each level and feeding back into the next.

**The BA's Role in the Cycle.** Regardless of the focus of the stakeholder, the importance of the role of the business analyst lies in continuously asking the question "why?" For example: "Why is either the requirement or design necessary to provide value to an enterprise and to facilitate the realization of an enterprise's goals and objectives?" (BABOK V3, Section 2.5). When a stakeholder presents a need or a solution to an assumed need, the BA uses activities found in Elicitation and Collaboration, Strategy Analysis, RADD, and Solution Evaluation to transform that request into a validated requirement or design.

**Practical Guidance.** Not every requirement will fit tidily into one or another of the classification categories. Software Requirements Essentials advises: "Debating exactly what to call a specific statement is not important. What's important is that the team recognizes the need, analyzes it, records it in an appropriate form and location, and builds whatever is necessary to satisfy it" (Wiegers & Hokanson, Ch. 1).

## Sub-module 1.5: Stakeholders in Business Analysis

---

### 1.5.1 Stakeholder Categories

**Definition.** A stakeholder is an individual or group that a business analyst is likely to interact with directly or indirectly. Each task in the BABOK includes a list of stakeholders who are likely to participate in the execution of that task or who will be affected by it (BABOK V3, Section 2.4). The BABOK does not mandate that these roles be filled for any given initiative. Any stakeholder can be a source of requirements, assumptions, or constraints.

**Generic Roles vs. Specific Titles.** The BABOK stakeholder list is not an exhaustive list of all possible stakeholder classifications. These are generic roles—in most cases there will be multiple specific titles found within each category. Similarly, a single individual may fill more than one role. When working with stakeholders, the BA should understand both the generic role (to ensure all perspectives are represented) and the specific individual (to tailor communication and engagement).

**The Eleven BABOK Stakeholder Roles (BABOK V3, Section 2.4):**

1. **Business Analyst** — Inherently a stakeholder in all business analysis activities. The BABOK presumes the BA is responsible and accountable for the execution of these activities. In some cases, the BA may also perform activities that fall under another stakeholder role.

2. **Customer** — Uses or may use products or services produced by the enterprise and may have contractual or moral rights that the enterprise is obliged to meet. Note: "customer" can mean different things in different contexts—an external purchasing customer or an internal customer who uses IT services. The BA should clarify this distinction early (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

3. **Domain Subject Matter Expert (SME)** — Any individual with in-depth knowledge of a topic relevant to the business need or solution scope. This role is often filled by end users, managers, process owners, legal staff, consultants, and others. Seven Steps provides extensive guidance on working with SMEs: "The SME provides critical understanding of the business: why things are done, how they are performed, what the results are, and ideas on what the business needs to be successful" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). Not all SMEs are equal in knowledge depth or willingness to participate; the BA must assess each SME's capability and engagement level.

4. **End User** — Stakeholders who directly interact with the solution. End users can include all participants in a business process or who use the product or solution. Seven Steps distinguishes between SMEs and users: a department manager may be an SME (understands business goals) but not a user (does not enter data); conversely, a data entry person may be a user but not an SME (lacks domain expertise) (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

5. **Implementation Subject Matter Expert** — Any stakeholder with specialized knowledge regarding implementation of one or more solution components. Common roles include: solution architect, developer, database administrator, information architect, usability analyst, trainer, organizational change consultant, project librarian, change manager, and configuration manager (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.5).

6. **Operational Support** — Responsible for the day-to-day management and maintenance of a system or product. Common roles include: operations analyst, product analyst, help desk, and release manager (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.6).

7. **Project Manager** — Responsible for managing the work required to deliver a solution that meets a business need, and for ensuring that the project's objectives are met while balancing scope, budget, schedule, resources, quality, and risk. Common roles include: project lead, technical lead, product manager, and team leader (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.7).

8. **Regulator** — Responsible for the definition and enforcement of standards. Standards can be imposed through legislation, corporate governance, audit standards, or standards defined by organizational centers of competency. Alternate roles include: government, regulatory bodies, and auditor (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.8).

9. **Sponsor** — Responsible for initiating the effort to define a business need and develop a solution that meets that need. They authorize the work to be performed and control the budget and scope for the initiative. Seven Steps emphasizes: "The first thing for you to learn about the sponsor is why they want this project carried out and what their success criteria will be" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). Communication with sponsors should be focused around accomplishments and action plans, not excuses—be brief, stay at a high level, and supply details only when necessary to support decision-making.

10. **Supplier** — A stakeholder outside the boundary of a given organization or organizational unit. Suppliers provide products or services to the organization and may have contractual or moral rights and obligations that must be considered. Alternate roles include: providers, vendors, and consultants (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.10).

11. **Tester** — Responsible for determining how to verify that the solution meets the requirements defined by the business analyst, as well as conducting the verification process. Testers also seek to ensure that the solution meets applicable quality standards and that the risk of defects or failures is understood and minimized. An alternate role is quality assurance analyst (BABOK V3, Section 2.4.11).

### 1.5.2 Stakeholder Analysis

**Purpose.** Stakeholder analysis is the process of identifying, categorizing, and understanding stakeholders to determine how best to engage them throughout the initiative. It is one of the first activities a BA performs when starting new work.

**Identification Techniques.** The BA should cast a wide net when identifying stakeholders. Seven Steps recommends a research-driven approach: review the organizational chart, examine who will be affected by the change, identify who has authority over the business areas in scope, ask existing stakeholders who else should be involved, and consider external stakeholders (customers, regulators, suppliers) who may be affected (Carkenord, Ch. 2). Software Requirements Essentials devotes Practice #4 to identifying and characterizing stakeholders, describing "The Quest for Stakeholders" as a systematic search for everyone who can influence or be affected by the initiative (Wiegers & Hokanson, Practice #4).

**Characterization Dimensions.** Once identified, stakeholders should be characterized along several dimensions:
- **Interest** — How much does this stakeholder care about the initiative and its outcomes?
- **Influence** — How much power does this stakeholder have to affect the initiative's direction, scope, or success?
- **Impact** — How much will this stakeholder be affected by the change?
- **Knowledge** — What domain expertise does this stakeholder possess that is relevant to the initiative?
- **Availability** — How much time can this stakeholder realistically dedicate to business analysis activities?

**Getting to Know Your SMEs.** Seven Steps provides practical advice for the BA's pre-engagement research on individual stakeholders (Carkenord, Ch. 2):
- Research the SME's position in the organization, reporting relationships, and team structure
- Ask PMs and other team members about prior experiences working with the SME
- Observe the SME's workspace for clues about working style, interests, and communication preferences
- Prepare specific questions about the types of information needed, so the SME can self-assess whether they are the right person to provide it
- Build the relationship as a whole-person connection, not just a transactional data extraction

**Empowered Decision Makers.** Software Requirements Essentials (Practice #5) emphasizes identifying not just stakeholders but specifically the empowered decision makers—those who have the authority to approve requirements, resolve conflicts, and make scope decisions. The BA must understand who makes the call, how they decide, and what happens following the decision. Without clear decision-making authority, requirements can stall indefinitely. Detailed engagement planning is covered in Module 2 (Sub-module 2.2).

---

### 1.5.3 Balancing Stakeholder Needs

**The Core Challenge.** Multiple stakeholders bring multiple—and often conflicting—needs, priorities, and definitions of value to any initiative. The BA's role is not to eliminate these differences but to surface, analyze, and help resolve them in a way that maximizes overall value delivery.

**Common Conflicts.** Seven Steps identifies several common stakeholder dynamics that require balancing:

- **Managers who do not understand their employees' work** — A department manager may be the primary stakeholder but may not know the specific details of how work processes are carried out or how systems function. Interviewing only the manager may result in incomplete requirements. The BA must identify the appropriate level of SME to provide detailed operational knowledge while still engaging the manager for strategic direction (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

- **PM and BA scope tension** — The PM is focused on delivering within scope, schedule, and budget. The BA is focused on ensuring the solution delivers value, which sometimes means the solution scope extends beyond the project scope. Creating a solid foundation between these two roles with clear objectives and a shared vision helps prevent this tension from derailing the initiative (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

- **Business vs. technical priorities** — Business stakeholders may prioritize features that drive revenue or customer satisfaction. Technical stakeholders may prioritize architectural quality, scalability, or debt reduction. Both perspectives have merit; the BA must facilitate negotiation that considers both short-term value delivery and long-term sustainability.

- **Inconsistent terminology** — Seven Steps warns that a simple misinterpretation of a common term like "customer" (external purchaser vs. internal IT user) can cause requirements elicitation to take longer than anticipated. The BA should clarify terminology and titles when they are used and be consistent throughout the initiative (Carkenord, Ch. 2).

**Strategies for Balancing.** The BA can employ several strategies to balance stakeholder needs:

- **Trace to business objectives** — When stakeholders disagree, ask which option better satisfies the business objectives. This provides an objective basis for resolution.
- **Present options with analysis** — Seven Steps illustrates this through a real-world example where the BA and PM presented sponsors with three options (proceed as planned, split the project, or run a pilot), each with risk analysis and value assessment, enabling a quick, informed decision (Carkenord, Ch. 2).
- **Identify the decision maker** — Software Requirements Essentials (Practice #5) emphasizes that someone must have the authority to resolve conflicts. The BA should identify this decision maker early and escalate appropriately when stakeholder negotiations reach an impasse.
- **Tailor communication** — Different stakeholders require different communication styles and levels of detail. Executives need concise summaries focused on value and decisions. Technical teams need detailed specifications. SMEs need to see their business processes accurately represented. The BA must tailor the format and level of detail to each audience.

**The Value of Both Roles.** Seven Steps makes a compelling case that having both a PM and a BA on a project leads to greater results: "Since a PM will manage risks on the project, sponsors need to be made aware that not having a BA is a risk factor to not only successful completion of the project, but also the success of the resulting solution" (Carkenord, Ch. 2). The PM ensures the project delivers on time and within budget; the BA ensures the solution delivers lasting value. Together, they ensure the change produces the desired value as designed—on time and within budget.
