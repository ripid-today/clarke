## Hexagram 33 — ䷠ Dùn (遯) / Retreat · Withdraw

**Trigrams:** Heaven (Qián ☰) over Mountain (Gèn ☶)
**Keywords:** retreat, withdrawal, strategic withdrawal, biding time, dark forces advancing

### Judgment
Retreat. Prosperous and smooth. The little: favorable to be steadfast and upright.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Mountain under Heaven, an image of Retreat. In correspondence with this, the superior person keeps inferior persons at a distance, with dignity but without ill will.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yin): Retreat at the tail. Adversity. Do not use anything. There is somewhere to go.
- **Line 2** (yin): Holding fast with hide of yellow ox. No one is able to loosen.
- **Line 3** (yang): Attached, retreats. There is illness. Adversity. Feed manservants and handmaidens: good fortune.
- **Line 4** (yang): Being fond of retreat: superior person — good fortune; little fellow — nay!
- **Line 5** (yang): Appropriate retreat. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
- **Line 6** (yang): Elegant retreat. Nothing is unfavorable.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua employs the image of a high mountain and faraway Heaven to expound the constructive meaning of retreat. When the dark forces spread and the brightness is too high to reach, one should retreat rather than compromise with the darkness. Retreat has a positive meaning — it is not flight or cowardice. To retreat is to preserve one's strength, waiting for the right time for future advance. The two yielding lines at the bottom represent dark forces multiplying and increasing in strength; the four solid lines symbolize the bright forces retreating constructively. Dùn is one of the twelve tidal gua, representing the sixth month of the Chinese lunar calendar.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Độn, là thối lui, là ẩn dật. "Thiên Địa bế, hiền nhơn ẩn" — when heaven and earth close, the virtuous retreat. Thi quẻ Độn thuộc về tháng sáu, âm trưởng dương tiêu. The commentary emphasizes that this is not escape from fear but a "chủ động" (active, purposeful) withdrawal — like a bee gathering honey that must work in darkness. The superior person keeps distance from petty people through dignity (bất ác nhi nghiêm — not through malice but through seriousness). The Vietnamese text references Gandhi as an embodiment of Hào Cửu Ngũ — decisive and absolute in withdrawing cooperation, yet without violence.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Dùn. Withdraw. Retreat does not mean surrender; this is a strategic withdrawal into safety and seclusion while you plan your next move. When the opponent is on the offensive and you have neither the power nor support to prevail, recede. Eventually the opponent will come to rest, and that is when you strike. The most profound degree of wisdom comes from knowing when to retreat. Curiously, line 3 of Hexagram 33 came up often in 2020 and 2021 during global shutdowns and quarantines.

## Hexagram 34 — ䷡ Dà Zhuàng (大壯) / Great Strength · Great Power

**Trigrams:** Thunder (Zhèn ☳) over Heaven (Qián ☰)
**Keywords:** great strength, power, righteousness essential, moral strength, caution at peak

### Judgment
Great Strength. Favorable to be steadfast and upright.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Thunder above Heaven. An image of Great Strength. In correspondence with this, the superior person does not act contrary to courtesy.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Strengthening the forward toes. Moving forward: misfortune. Be sincere and truthful.
- **Line 2** (yang): Being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yang): Little fellow uses strength; superior person uses nothing. Being steadfast: adversity. A ram butting against a hedge entangles its horns.
- **Line 4** (yang): Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. Regret vanishes. The hedge falls apart, no more entanglement. Strength at the spokes of a big cart.
- **Line 5** (yin): Lose a ram in the field. No regret.
- **Line 6** (yin): A ram butts against a hedge, unable to go backward, unable to go forward. Nothing is favorable. Difficulty, then good fortune.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua expounds the principle of how to use one's great strength. The main theme is that one should not become too self-willed and opinionated. To the ancient Chinese, great strength should not merely appear as physical strength — moral strength was more important and powerful than physical strength. Relying on physical strength, one's achievement could not endure. The first, third, and topmost lines warn that those who rely too much on strength lose their equanimity and find nothing favorable. Dà Zhuàng is one of the twelve tidal gua, representing the second month of the Chinese lunar calendar.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Quẻ Đại Tráng, là 4 dương tịnh tiến, Dương trưởng Âm tiêu. The commentary warns: "Đắc ý thường tư thất ý thời" — at the peak of success, remember the time of failure. The Vietnamese commentary emphasizes Dịch's law of reversal: when yang is at its strongest, a single additional yang element turns to yin. "Phúc đó, họa đó" — fortune and misfortune lie beside each other. The superior person, seeing the thunder in heaven, does not act against ritual propriety (phi lễ phật lý).

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Dà Zhuàng. Great Power. Hexagram 34 denotes great personal power and accumulating an abundance of resources to further your endeavor. If Hexagram 33 was about retreating and laying low until you're ready, then Hexagram 34 is the Oracle telling you you're ready — strike at full strength. Hexagram 34 corresponds with the vernal equinox and symbolizes progress, growth, and fertile times. The narrative references Wang Hai, the legendary wanderer who attained great prosperity as an outsider in the kingdom of Yi, arousing envy and conspiracy.

## Hexagram 35 — ䷢ Jìn (晉) / Proceeding Forward · Advancement · Progress

**Trigrams:** Fire (Lí ☲) over Earth (Kūn ☷)
**Keywords:** progress, advancement, rising sun, cultivating virtue, gradual advance

### Judgment
Proceeding Forward. Lord Kang is honored with numerous horses. In a single day, thrice received at interview.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
The Light comes forth over the Earth. An image of Proceeding Forward. In correspondence with this, the superior person cultivates his virtues and brightens them by himself.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yin): Proceeding forward, being held back. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. No confidence. Being calm and unhurried: no fault.
- **Line 2** (yin): Proceeding forward with worry. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. Receiving this great blessing from his grandmother.
- **Line 3** (yin): From the multitude obtain trust. Regret vanishes.
- **Line 4** (yang): Proceeding forward like a rat. Being steadfast: adversity.
- **Line 5** (yin): Regret vanishes. Lose or gain, stop worrying. Going forward: good fortune. Nothing is unfavorable.
- **Line 6** (yang): Proceeding forward into his horn. Appropriate to conquer one's own domain. Adversity: good fortune. No fault. Being steadfast: humiliation.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua expounds the principle of proceeding. If one's strength is great, one is able to progress and contribute one's talent to society — like the rising sun shining upon all beings on Earth. In the process of proceeding, one's motivation should be pure. Proceeding should be stable, gradual, and steady. Both Wilhelm and Blofeld translate Jìn as Progress. The host of this gua is the yielding line at the fifth place, in the middle of the upper gua Fire (Li). The image: a rising sun over the earth.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Hỏa Địa Tấn — the sun rises above earth, an image of brilliant progress. The commentary notes the reference to "Lord Kang" (Khang hầu) who receives great honors — a model of virtuous advancement. Quẻ Tấn contrasts with Thăng (46): Tấn is effortless advance like the sun rising naturally without effort; Thăng is like a tree pushing through soil with determined effort.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Jìn. Advancement. The prognostication of the first line speaks to what you can do proactively to advance your own position. The second line carries divine intervention. Hexagram 35 is first about what you alone can do to advance.

## Hexagram 36 — ䷣ Míng Yí (明夷) / Brilliance Injured · Darkening of the Light

**Trigrams:** Earth (Kūn ☷) over Fire (Lí ☲)
**Keywords:** hidden brilliance, persecution, inner light, endurance under darkness, tao guang yang hui

### Judgment
Brilliance Injured. Favorable to be steadfast and upright in hardship.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Brightness has sunk under the Earth. An image of Brilliance Injured. In correspondence with this, the superior person remains in harmony with the multitude. Covering his brilliance, yet his light is still shining.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Brilliance injured is flying, drooping its wings. The sage evades, three days ignores eating. There is somewhere to go. The host gossips.
- **Line 2** (yin): Brilliance injured, injured in the left thigh. Needs aid, a strong horse. Good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yang): Brilliance injured hunting in the south. Captured the great chief. Act not with undue haste; be steadfast and upright.
- **Line 4** (yin): Entering the left belly, knowing the heart of the injured brilliance. Quit the dwelling place.
- **Line 5** (yin): Ji Zi's brilliance injured. Favorable to be steadfast and upright.
- **Line 6** (yin): No brightness, only darkness. At first, ascend to Heaven. Afterward, fall to earth.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua expounds the truth of hiding one's brilliance and biding one's time during an extremely difficult situation. The Chinese maxim tao guang yang hui — hide one's light and bide one's time — is the connotation of Brilliance Injured. This gua is the inverse of Proceeding Forward (35). The structure — Earth above, Fire below — shows the setting sun, brightness hidden by the earth. This was the situation of King Wen when imprisoned by the Tyrant of Shang. Both King Wen (line 3) and Ji Zi (line 5) are cited as exemplars of remaining steadfast and upright during times of darkness. The topmost line — the Tyrant of Shang — ascended to Heaven but fell to earth because he lost the code of conduct.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Địa Hỏa Minh Di. The commentary draws heavily on Laozi's teaching: "Tri kỳ bạch, thủ kỳ hắc; tri kỳ hùng, thủ kỳ thư" — know the light within, but outwardly maintain darkness. The reference to Laozi advising Confucius captures the essence: "thông minh duệ trí, thủ chi dĩ ngu" — if you are supremely intelligent, act as though ignorant. The inner trigram (Lý) is bright as the sun; the outer trigram (Khôn) conceals it. The Battle of Muye is referenced in this hexagram.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Míng Yí. Darkening of the Light. Hexagram 36 is a didactic narrative of a tyrant consumed by darkness and evil, suppressing his people. Virtue goes underground, and any who are virtuous must conceal that virtue to stay alive. Every time you see the word "light" or "darkening of the light," consider the many facets of what light means. When you see Fire pushing into Earth in Hexagram 36 in a geopolitical reading, it predicts war brought on by self-serving leaders. The story of Ji Zi (Jizi/Gija) — the virtuous brother of the corrupt last king of Shang — is central to this hexagram. Hexagram 36 includes a reference to the Battle of Muye in line 3.

## Hexagram 37 — ䷤ Jiā Rén (家人) / Household · The Family

**Trigrams:** Wind (Xùn ☴) over Fire (Lí ☲)
**Keywords:** family, household, proper roles, harmony, woman's virtue, governance begins at home

### Judgment
Household. Favorable for a woman to be steadfast and upright.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
The wind comes forth from the fire. An image of Household. In correspondence with this, the superior person is substantial in his words and consistent in his deeds.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Take precautions within a household. Regret vanishes.
- **Line 2** (yin): Making no arbitrary decision, taking no irresolute action. In charge of feeding the household, being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yang): The household, grumbling, grumbling. Regret for adversity: good fortune. Women and children, joking, joking. Ends in humiliation.
- **Line 4** (yin): Enrich the household. Great good fortune.
- **Line 5** (yang): The king influences his household. Do not worry. Good fortune.
- **Line 6** (yang): Being sincere and upright, with dignity, ends in good fortune.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua expounds the principle of managing a household. In ancient China, filial piety and respect were considered the root of all morality. The atmosphere of a household should be harmonious and smooth — neither too stern nor too indulgent. The image Wind above Fire: the wind springs forth from the fire, the wind of harmony fed by the flames of love. The ancient sages always applied the principle of managing a household to governing a country. One result of King Wen's release from prison was returning home and encouraging families and careers. The two hosts are the solid line at fifth place (husband) and the yielding line at second place (wife).

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Phong Hỏa Gia Nhơn. Commentary: "Số Trời đầu ở 1, số Đất đầu ở 2; bởi Âm không đầu, nên phải theo Dương." The Vietnamese commentary connects this hexagram to cosmic order — the arrangement of heaven and earth numbers as the basis for family roles. Discusses the complementary roles of husband and wife as extensions of yang and yin. Traditionalists interpret Hexagram 37 as particularly auspicious for women, and the Oracle counsels following the woman's direction.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Jiā Rén. The Family. At the heart of Hexagram 37 is the political philosophy that the strength and power of a nation pivots upon the social and economic status of its society's women. Traditionalists interpret the hexagram as moral instruction on a woman's place and influence in the family — women hold the clan together. In readings for a man, the Oracle counsels following the woman in the matter at hand.

## Hexagram 38 — ䷥ Kuí (睽) / Diversity · Opposition

**Trigrams:** Fire (Lí ☲) over Lake (Duì ☱)
**Keywords:** opposition, diversity, seeking harmony amid difference, estrangement, reconciliation

### Judgment
Diversity. Little things: good fortune.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Fire above, Lake below. An image of Diversity. In correspondence with this, the superior person seeks common ground on major issues while reserving differences on minor ones.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Regret vanishes. Lose a horse, do not chase. Of its own accord, returns. Meet with evil people. No fault.
- **Line 2** (yang): Meet lord in a narrow lane. No fault.
- **Line 3** (yin): Seeing a wagon dragged, one's ox pulled, as if one's forehead were tattooed and nose cut off. Without a beginning, there is an end.
- **Line 4** (yang): Diversity isolated. Meets the initiator. Fused sincerity and truthfulness. Adversity. No fault.
- **Line 5** (yin): Regret vanishes. Clansmen respond as if biting soft meat. Going forward, what mistake?
- **Line 6** (yang): Diversity isolated. See a pig covered with dirt, a wagon carrying devils. First draw the bow, then lay it aside. Not an invader, but a suitor. Going forward, encounter the rain, then good fortune.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
Diversity is natural and unavoidable. The key is in seeking harmony. To the Chinese, all diversity can be harmonized, no matter whether it is between household members, society members, or nations. Both gua Household (37) and Diversity (38) are made up of Lake and Fire, but in reversed positions. In Household, the centripetal force holds the family together; here, the centrifugal force diversifies. The clue lies in one's attitude — if both sides are willing to come together in sincerity and truthfulness, no problem cannot be solved.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Hỏa Trạch Khuê. Commentary notes the adage "Les contraires s'attirent" (opposites attract) and references this as a law of utmost importance for I Ching students. "Thương nhau lắm, cắn nhau đau!" — the more you love, the more you bite. The Vietnamese commentary cites Aurobindo Ghose: "Quand nous avons dépassé les savoirs, alors nous avons la Connaissance. La Raison fut une Aide, le Raison est l'Entrave." Hexagram 38 can also be the sign of an iconoclast, a perpetual skeptic. In statecraft, it prognosticates the brink of civil war or threats of secession.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Kuí. Opposition. The final line of Hexagram 38 expresses a temporary misunderstanding that at first blush appeared to be opposition; but once the matter was clarified, the opposition is immediately resolved and found to be a relation conducted in good faith. In a love reading, Hexagram 38 can indicate a relationship where opposites attract, and that will be both what they love most about each other, and what fuels most of their arguments.

## Hexagram 39 — ䷦ Jiǎn (蹇) / Hardship · An Impasse · Obstruction

**Trigrams:** Water (Kǎn ☵) over Mountain (Gèn ☶)
**Keywords:** hardship, obstruction, difficulty, introspection, seeking great persons, southwest favorable

### Judgment
Hardship. Favorable to the southwest. Unfavorable to the northeast. Favorable to see a great person. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Water on the Mountain. An image of Hardship. In correspondence with this, the superior person is introspective to cultivate his virtue.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yin): Going forward: hardship. Coming back: praise.
- **Line 2** (yin): King and minister, hardship, hardship. Not for one's own affairs.
- **Line 3** (yang): Going forward: hardship. Come back instead.
- **Line 4** (yin): Going forward: hardship. Coming back: union.
- **Line 5** (yang): Great hardship. Friends come.
- **Line 6** (yin): Going forward: hardship. Coming back: a great achievement. Good fortune. Favorable to see a great person.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
Water above Mountain is an image of hardship following hardship. There is no way to totally avoid hardship in one's life. Hardship should be overcome; calamity can be prevented. Overcoming hardship depends on the correct time, situation, and companions — in Chinese terms Heaven, earth, and human beings, the three primary elements. The host of the gua is the solid line at the fifth place. In times of hardship, if the time is not auspicious one should keep still. Any premature advance will lead to possible danger.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Thủy Sơn Kiển. The commentary discusses the story of Hàn Tín and Bái Công — two equally ambitious men who inevitably came into conflict. "Hai con chó con, đang đùa giỡn với nhau rất thân thiết... nếu ta thấy vậy mà thích thú, thì chớ có liệng cho chúng miếng xương vụn" — two dogs playing happily together; don't throw them a bone or they'll fight over it. Quẻ Kiển emphasizes self-reflection: Mencius says when others do not respond positively, look inward and examine oneself. The theme of southwest (positive) and northeast (negative) reappears here.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Jiǎn. An Impasse. The impasses of Hexagram 39 are in the conflicting flow of qi of the paired trigrams. Mountain wants to be still and is solid; Water wants to flow. The theme of southwest being positive and northeast being negative appears again.

## Hexagram 40 — ䷧ Jiě (解) / Relief · Release of Tension · Deliverance

**Trigrams:** Thunder (Zhèn ☳) over Water (Kǎn ☵)
**Keywords:** relief, release, deliverance, thunderstorm clearing the air, pardoning mistakes, fresh start

### Judgment
Relief. Favorable in the southwest. Nowhere to go — come back, return to normal. Good fortune. Somewhere to go — no delay: good fortune.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Rolling Thunder with heavy Rain. An image of Relief. In correspondence with this, the superior person pardons mistakes and deals gently with misdeeds.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yin): No fault.
- **Line 2** (yang): In the field three foxes are caught. Obtain a golden arrow. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yin): Carrying a burden, riding in a carriage, tempting robbers to come. Being steadfast: humiliation.
- **Line 4** (yang): Removing your big toe, friends come. Be sincere and truthful.
- **Line 5** (yin): Superior person relieves hardship: good fortune. Be sincere and truthful to the little fellow.
- **Line 6** (yin): The prince is engaged in shooting a falcon on top of a high city wall. Hits it. Nothing is unfavorable.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua is the inverse of Hardship (39). After hardship is relieved, timing is important. If there is nowhere to go, returning will be propitious. If there is somewhere to go, acting immediately brings good fortune. The gua has two hosts: the yielding line at the fifth place (a humble, gentle king) and the solid line at the second place (a firm, strong official). They mutually support each other. The images — the fox, the robber, and the falcon — suggest that even during a time of relief one should remain cautious. The thunderstorm symbol: "After a thunderstorm, the sky becomes clearer."

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Lôi Thủy Giải. The Vietnamese commentary presents multiple line glosses. "Phụ 負, là bọn chuyên về việc khuân vác; thừa 乘 là ngồi chễm" — carrying a burden while riding in a carriage is a fundamental contradiction. "Giải nhi mẫu" means resolving by cutting (line 2 gloss). "Xạ 射, là bắn; Chuẩn là một loại chim rất mạnh và dữ; cao dung 高墉 — the high city wall."

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Jiě. Release of Tension. The resulting hexagram in a sample divination is Thunder over Water, or Hexagram 40, with the fourth line as the changing line.

## Hexagram 41 — ䷨ Sǔn (損) / Decreasing · Debilitation

**Trigrams:** Mountain (Gèn ☶) over Lake (Duì ☱)
**Keywords:** decrease, reduction, self-discipline, giving, controlling anger, balancing excess and deficiency

### Judgment
Decreasing. Being sincere and truthful: supreme good fortune, no fault. Appropriate to be steadfast and upright. Favorable to have somewhere to go. How is it to be used? Two bamboo trays can be used for offering.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
At the foot of the Mountain, there is a Lake. It is an image of Decreasing. In correspondence with this, the superior person controls his anger and restrains his desire.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Suspend one's own affairs. Hurry forward, no fault. Weigh how much one can decrease.
- **Line 2** (yang): Favorable to be steadfast and upright. Moving forward: misfortune. Without decreasing, increase.
- **Line 3** (yin): Three people walk together; then decrease by one. One walks alone, then finds a friend.
- **Line 4** (yin): Decrease one's ailment, hasten to help. There is rejoicing, no fault.
- **Line 5** (yin): Probably being increased. A tortoise worth ten pairs of shells cannot be refused. Supreme good fortune.
- **Line 6** (yang): Without decreasing, increase. No fault. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. Favorable to have somewhere to go. Obtain subordinates without consideration of their own households.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
The theme of this gua is the law of balance between decreasing and increasing, giving and receiving. What is excessive should be decreased; what is insufficient should be increased. This is the way of the universe. To decrease what is lower is to increase what is above. King Wen performed a grand ceremony using just two bamboo trays instead of the traditional eight — offering a simple sacrifice with truthfulness and sincerity — as an example of economy. Mountain over Lake: alchemically, this is the power to repel, the alchemical phase of putrefaction.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Sơn Trạch Tổn. Commentary notes the story of Quý Trát who resolved to give a treasured sword to the King of Từ — but arrived after the king had died. He nonetheless hung the sword on the king's grave, honoring his inner intention. Also noted: "Thập bằng chi quy" — a giant sacred tortoise, symbolizing that Heaven itself wishes to increase you; this blessing cannot be refused. Commentary also warns about the old custom of "Chồng Chúa, Vợ Tôi" and "Trai năm Thê bảy Thiếp" as being harmful to true love.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Sǔn. Debilitation. You remain protected by the Divine. Hexagram 41 can be a sign of one with an external aspect of the self that is visibly debilitated but internally is strong. Mountain over Lake is the power to repel, the alchemical phase of putrefaction. Haindl assigns Hexagram 41 to the Eight of Cups in tarot.

## Hexagram 42 — ䷩ Yì (益) / Increasing · Burgeoning

**Trigrams:** Wind (Xùn ☴) over Thunder (Zhèn ☳)
**Keywords:** increase, gain, benefit, growth, following good when seen, correcting faults swiftly

### Judgment
Increasing. Favorable to have somewhere to go. Favorable to cross great rivers.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Wind and Thunder support each other. An image of Increasing. In correspondence with this, the superior person follows the good when he sees it, and corrects his fault when he finds it.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yang): Favorable to engage in conducting great accomplishments. Supreme good fortune, no fault.
- **Line 2** (yin): Probably being increased. A tortoise worth ten pairs of shells cannot be refused. Being perseveringly steadfast and upright: good fortune. The king is engaged in presenting to the Lord of Heaven. Good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yin): Being increased. Engaging in unfortunate events. No fault. Being sincere and truthful, walking the central path, reporting to the lord with jade gui.
- **Line 4** (yin): Walking the central path, reporting to the lord. The lord follows. Favorable to use this as the basis to move a capital.
- **Line 5** (yang): With sincerity and truthfulness, benefiting their hearts. No need to ask. Supreme good fortune. Being sincere and truthful, my virtue will be favored.
- **Line 6** (yang): No increase, but a strike. Not keeping the heart steady: misfortune.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
Increasing is the inverse of Decreasing (41). To decrease what is above is to increase what is below — the joy of the people is boundless. Wind over Thunder, when wind and thunder support each other, their energy is doubled. The superior person follows the good as quickly as the wind and corrects faults as firmly as thunder. Increasing comes from Hindrance (12): when the topmost solid line of Hindrance moves to the bottom, it becomes Increasing. King Wen believed it was favorable for the government to overcome hindrance for the public welfare. Wind over Thunder is the momentum that overpowers the opposition, and thus it is change toward increase, gain, and escalation.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Phong Lôi Ích. Quẻ Phong Lôi Ích gồm 2 quẻ Tốn thượng và Chấn hạ. Tốn, là Gió; Chấn, là Sấm sét. Hai thứ này phụ ích nhau: Gió càng to; thì Sấm sét lại càng to. A talisman featuring Hexagram 42 is used to increase wealth and prosperity, and to channel universal qi that will move you toward increase and material gains.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Yì. Burgeoning. The second line includes the same ten tortoise shells reference from the fifth line of Hexagram 41 — an affirmation that you are blessed by Heaven. Haindl assigns Hexagram 42 to the Nine of Cups (the proverbial wish card) in tarot. Hexagram 42 is also used in talismans for wealth and prosperity.

## Hexagram 43 — ䷪ Guài (夬) / Eliminating · Decisive Action

**Trigrams:** Lake (Duì ☱) over Heaven (Qián ☰)
**Keywords:** decisive action, eliminating evil, resolution, declaring at court, resoluteness, breakthrough

### Judgment
Eliminating. Declaring at the king's court; sincerely howling: there is adversity. Notify one's own city: unfavorable to use arms, favorable to have somewhere to go.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image

The host of the gua is the yielding line at the second place: "Beginning: good fortune, for the yielding is in the central." The ancient Chinese remind us: to undertake a project is difficult, but to maintain what has been achieved is even more so. Hexagram 63 is assigned to the Four of Wands in Haindl tarot (completion of a great work).

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Thủy Hỏa Ký Tế. Commentary discusses the rise and decline of the Shang dynasty — their golden age, then decay into excess and tyrant rule. Emperor Gao Zong's conquests (line 3) represent the Shang at their peak. The "caulking the leak" (line 4) represents desperate measures. The distinction between the eastern neighbor (materialistic, excessive offering) and the western neighbor (sincere, modest offering) — the western neighbor is King Wen, the eastern is the Tyrant.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Jì Jì. After the Ending. Hexagram 63 (After the Ending) is assigned to the Four of Wands in Haindl tarot, indicating completion of a great work. Per Image and Number scholars, Hexagram 22 is alchemically related to Hexagram 63 — if 22 appears in a reading, there is an implied prediction of change to 63. A passage in Taoist scriptures instructs on using Hexagram 63 to subdue ghosts and demons.

## Hexagram 64 — ䷿ Wèi Jì (未濟) / Not Yet Fulfilled · Toward an End · Before Completion

**Trigrams:** Fire (Lí ☲) over Water (Kǎn ☵)
**Keywords:** not yet complete, transition, beginning of new cycle, promise, the fox crossing the river, disorder becoming order

### Judgment
Not Yet Fulfilled. Prosperous and smooth. Little fox, almost across the river, wets its tail. Nothing is unfavorable.
<!-- src: huang -->

### The Image
Fire over Water. An image of Not Yet Fulfilled. In correspondence with this, the superior person discriminates carefully the nature of things and keeps each of them in its proper position.
<!-- src: huang -->

### Lines (Moving Lines)
- **Line 1** (yin): Wetting one's tail. Humiliation.
- **Line 2** (yang): Dragging one's wheels. Being steadfast and upright: good fortune.
- **Line 3** (yin): Not yet fulfilled. Moving forward: misfortune. Favorable to cross great rivers.
- **Line 4** (yang): Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. Regret vanishes. Attacks the Gui Fang like thunder. Three years: rewards come from a great kingdom.
- **Line 5** (yin): Being steadfast and upright: good fortune. No regret. The light of a superior person, being sincere and truthful: good fortune.
- **Line 6** (yang): Being sincere and truthful while drinking wine. No fault. Immersing one's head, being sincere and truthful, loses correctness.

### Alfred Huang's Commentary <!-- src: huang -->
This gua ends the sixty-four gua but the principle of change continues without end. Not Yet Fulfilled is the inverse and opposite of Already Fulfilled (63). Here all yang lines are at yin places, and all yin lines are at yang places — no perfect equilibrium, but there is balance. This is the beginning of a new cycle, a transition from disorder to order. Already Fulfilled is merely the fulfillment of a certain stage; where there is an ending, there is also a beginning. The I Ching starts with Qian the Initiating and ends with Wei Ji Not Yet Fulfilled — when the cycle ends, another begins. In this way the cycles of change and development repeat endlessly. The structure (Fire over Water) echoes the diagram that is numerologically significant: 6 + 4 = 10, the final completion number.

### Vietnamese Commentary [vi] <!-- src: viet -->
Hỏa Thủy Vị Tế. Commentary on the final hexagram: "Số phận của nhà Thương đã thành rồi (Ký Tế). Số phận của nhà Chu chưa thành (Vị Tế)" — the destiny of Shang was already fulfilled; the destiny of Zhou was not yet fulfilled. The little fox crossing the river: already nearly across, but wets its tail through impatience or inattention. The ending is not the end — it is the beginning of a new becoming.

### Wen Benebell's Commentary <!-- src: wen -->
Name: Wèi Jì. Toward an End. The diagram of Fire over Water is Hexagram 64, and this was the last and sixty-fourth diagram received by King Wen. 6 + 4 = 10, which is the final completion number. The Lo Shu arrangement shows how the completion number 10 transcends directionality. There is no true ending in the I Ching — the cycle of change continues without end. The sixth yang line's final caution: "drinking wine and wetting one's head — one knows no self-control."
