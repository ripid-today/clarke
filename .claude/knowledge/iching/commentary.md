## Historical Origins

The I Ching (易經, Yìjīng) — the Book of Changes — has its roots in a lineage of four foundational sages credited by Chinese tradition with its authorship. By the Song dynasty (AD 960–1279), these four contributions were cemented:

1. **Fu Xi (伏羲, c. 2600 BC)** — Mythic culture hero who devised the eight primary trigrams (Ba Gua). According to legend, Fu Xi and Nüwā were the sole survivors of a Great Flood. Fu Xi harnessed the duality of yin and yang and received knowledge of the trigrams as a divine gift. His arrangement of the eight trigrams is called the Early Heaven Ba Gua (先天八卦). The eight trigrams became an early form of writing so that those on Earth could communicate with the spirits in Heaven. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

2. **King Wen of Zhou (周文王, c. 1100 BC)** — Around 1050 BC, King Wen was imprisoned by King Zhou of Shang for seven years. During his imprisonment, he combined Fu Xi's eight trigrams in pairs to form sixty-four hexagrams, and wrote a brief oracle text (guací, 卦辭) — known as the Decision or Judgment — for each. He also brought together Fu Xi's Early Heaven Ba Gua and Yu the Great's Lo Shu magic square to create the Later Heaven Ba Gua (後天八卦). His version of the text is called the Zhouyi (周易), the Changes of Zhou. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md, 09_wen_iching.md)*

3. **The Duke of Zhou (周公, c. 1046 BC)** — Younger brother of King Wu (King Wen's son), the Duke of Zhou wrote the Yao Texts (爻辭, Yáocí), adding oracular commentary to each of the six lines within every hexagram. He is credited with memorializing the historical chronicles of the Zhou dynasty within the hexagram lines. Historical figures, battles (including the Battle of Muye), and court personages are alluded to throughout the text. The Duke of Zhou is later canonized as the Chinese god of dreams. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md, 09_wen_iching.md)*

4. **Confucius (孔子, 551–479 BC)** — Five hundred years after King Wen, Confucius is said to have added the Ten Wings (十翼, Shíyì), a set of ten commentary texts that transformed the Zhouyi from a divination handbook into a philosophical and ethical classic. Tradition holds that Confucius said at age 70: "If some years were added to my life, I would dedicate fifty years to study of the Book of I, and then I might come to be without great fault." *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md, 09_wen_iching.md)*

### Earlier Traditions

Before the Zhouyi, two earlier Books of Changes existed: the I of the Xia dynasty (c. 2005–1766 BC), called Lian Shan, which began with the hexagram Gen (Mountain); and the I of the Shang dynasty (c. 1766–1122 BC), called Gui Cang, which began with Kun (Earth). Only fragments of these survive. The Zhouyi is the further evolution of both. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The Shang dynasty (1766–1122 BC) practiced divination by cracking tortoise shells and ox bones with a hot iron rod and reading the resulting cracks. The oldest known Chinese writing is found on these oracle bones, and many contain three-line trigrams and six-line hexagrams. The Zhou dynasty replaced oracle bone divination with yarrow stalk divination using the hexagrams. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md, 09_wen_iching.md)*

The original text of the I Ching contains fewer than five thousand Chinese characters — less than forty pages in translation. After Confucius's commentaries were written, the book became known as the I Ching (with Ching meaning "classic" or "sacred text"). *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

## The Ten Wings (Shí Yì, 十翼)

The Ten Wings are ten commentary texts appended to the Zhouyi. Traditionally attributed to Confucius, modern scholarship holds they were authored by Confucian scholars over the Warring States period (403–221 BC). The Chinese believe the I Ching depends on the Ten Wings to fly — without them, the text cannot be fully understood. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The Ten Wings as identified in the Alfred Huang tradition:

| Wing | Chinese Name | Content |
|------|-------------|---------|
| 1st & 2nd | Commentary on the Decision (彖傳, Tuàn Zhuàn) | Explains the meaning of King Wen's Judgment/Decision for each hexagram; often gives the reason for the choice of words and expounds the attributes of the hexagram |
| 3rd & 4th | Commentary on the Symbols (象傳, Xiàng Zhuàn) | Starts from the symbolic meaning of the linear complexes (the two trigrams); indicates basic application to human, social, or cosmic situations; often has remarkable beauty of expression |
| 5th & 6th | The Great Treatise (大傳, Dà Zhuàn), also called Hsi T'zu or Appended Judgments (繫辭, Xì Cí) | A general philosophical introduction to the book as a whole; the most expansive of the Wings; describes the cosmological basis of the I Ching |
| 7th | Commentary on the Words of the Text (文言, Wén Yán) | A very early commentary; only fragments concerning the first two hexagrams (Qian and Kun) are extant |
| 8th | Discussion of the Trigrams (說卦, Shuō Guà) | Interprets the two primary trigrams making up each hexagram and their symbolic values |
| 9th | Sequence of the Gua (序卦, Xù Guà) | Explains the sequence of the hexagrams; gives interpretations of their names |
| 10th | Miscellaneous Notes on the Gua (雜卦, Zá Guà) | Gives brief definitions of each hexagram, often drawing contrasts between pairs |

The Hsiang (Image) texts from the 3rd/4th Wing are of particular note. They indicate basic human, social, or cosmic applications from the hexagram's trigram symbolism, and often contain historically grounded or symbolically forceful counsels addressed to "the superior man." *(Source: 10_wilhelm_iching.md)*

The yarrow stalk method described in the Great Treatise (5th Wing) is the classical divination procedure: beginning with fifty stalks, setting one aside to represent the commencement of Tai Chi from the void, then dividing, counting by fours, and repeating the process eighteen times to obtain all six lines of a hexagram. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

## Daoist Interpretive Tradition

The I Ching is not itself Taoist, but it inspired Taoism — and Taoism, in turn, became one of the two great traditions through which the text has been most deeply read. In the Taoist scriptural tradition, the I Ching is ranked first among the Three Mysteries (三玄, Sān Xuán). *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The Taoist reading of the I Ching is associated with the concept of the Tao as the natural flow of qi through the universe. A key Taoist interpreter of the I Ching was **Wang Bi (王弼, AD 226–249)**, representing what later came to be called the Rationalist school. Wang Bi interpreted the I Ching through the lens of the Tao Te Ching, treating each hexagram as a fluid, interconnected whole and the Ten Wings as determinative of how the Zhouyi should be read. He used Taoist philosophy — specifically the principles of the Tao Te Ching — to explain the metaphysical correspondences within the text. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Wang Bi's key Taoist principle was to avoid imposing moral judgments on external events, seeing them instead as the natural course of the Tao. He did not ascribe sentience to the Tao: "The Tao that an I Ching divination detects and gives a reading on is just 'where things come from.'" In his view, the six-line code of each hexagram is an analogous diagram or microcosmic model of a transformation occurring in the cosmos; divination is a calculation method for detecting what change or transformation is occurring. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

A more esoteric Taoist tradition is found in the **School of Mystery (Xuan Xue, 玄學)**, a Taoist school of esoteric philosophical thought followed by a small intellectual elite. The School of Mystery advocated that humans should live intuitively in accordance with their natural inclinations, free from social rules, while using logic, rationalism, and rigorous scholarship to unveil universal mysteries. It ran alongside yet also connected to Confucianism. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The **Gua Qi school (卦氣學)**, rising in the Western Han (202 BC–AD 9), represents another Taoist-influenced tradition, holding that every hexagram emanates with a particular qi life force (卦氣, guàqì) that influences the human condition, much as specific acupuncture points connect to channels of qi in the body. Under Gua Qi theory, the sixty-four hexagrams represent every channel of qi in the universe. Yin and yang qualities of qi form combinations corresponding to physical and natural laws: heat, cold, shape, size, sound, and numerical values all relate to specific patterns of qi. The hexagrams are mapped onto seasonal cycles and the twenty-four solar terms of the lunisolar calendar. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The **Yì wěi (易緯)** — a collection of eight esoteric apocryphal texts dated to the Han dynasty — represents the esoteric Confucian/Taoist intersection. These texts discuss the qi of hexagrams, geomancy, feng shui, directional deities, spirits of the underworld, ceremonial magic for controlling disasters, and the Lo Shu magic square. For adepts, the Oracle was a master key for ascending to the domain of the immortals. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The central Taoist maxim threading through I Ching study: "This world belongs to all (天下為公, Tiān xià wèi gōng) — all who are worthy and capable, all who are trustworthy and in harmony with nature." Sovereignty is earned, not inherited. Spiritual awakening is cultivated, not destined. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

## Confucian Interpretive Tradition

Confucianism became the dominant lens through which the I Ching has been read for most of its history. The I Ching was canonized around 136 BC as the first of the Five Classics, and since then interpretation has been largely inextricable from Confucian principles. China's imperial civil service examinations required knowledge of the I Ching; the literati spent their lives refining their understanding of it. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Confucius emphasized the cultivation of morality, interpersonal relationships, social contracts, loyalty, and filial piety. He reframed the older divination vocabulary: the word "貞" (zhēn), which originally meant "to divine, holding ritual offerings to the gods and ancestors," came to mean "purity, perseverance, chastity, loyalty" under Confucian influence. Similarly, "亨" (hēng), which originally meant ritual sacrifice and divination, was reframed as one of the Confucian four virtues: 元亨利貞 (yuán hēng lì zhēn) — to be perfected, to be accomplished, to always advance, and to always persevere. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The core Confucian aspiration within the I Ching is the junzi (君子, jūnzǐ) — the person of perfected virtuous character, often translated as "superior man" or "gentleman." In practice, every Image text (3rd/4th Wing) addresses the junzi with specific moral counsel derived from the hexagram's symbolism. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md, 10_wilhelm_iching.md)*

### The Rationalist School (Wang Bi) vs. The Originalist School (Zhu Xi)

Two contrasting approaches mark the history of Confucian I Ching scholarship:

**Wang Bi (王弼, AD 226–249) — Rationalist:** Emphasized the practical use of the I Ching to solve immediate worldly affairs. Treated the Ten Wings as determinative of how the Zhouyi should be interpreted. Focused on political value; the I Ching was for scholar-sages. His approach was standard through the Tang and Song dynasties (AD 618–1279), and imperial examinations tested applicants on his commentaries. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

**Zhu Xi (朱熹, AD 1130–1200) — Originalist / Neo-Confucian:** Emphasized the experience of divination as an experience of the Divine itself, and divination as a journey in self-cultivation. His school, Benyi (本義), or Originalist approach, sought to restore the I Ching to its original intent as understood by its four forefathers. Crucially, Zhu Xi believed the I Ching was originally created for all people — farmers, artisans, merchants, and peasants alike, not only the literati. He wrote: "When the sages created the I Ching, it originally was to cause people to engage in divination, in order to decide what was permissible or not in their behavior, and thereby to teach people to be good." The Originalist approach studies the Zhouyi separately from the Ten Wings. By the seventeenth century, his approach became the accepted paradigm. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

By the Yuan dynasty (1279–1368), over seven hundred different schools of Yì Xué (易學, I Ching scholarship) could be catalogued, demonstrating the text's extraordinary interpretive range.

### The New Text vs. Old Text Controversy

A further Confucian schism concerned which version of the I Ching to use. The New Text school (Han dynasty orthodox) characterized Confucius as a messianic figure and treated the classics as sacred religious texts. The Old Text school (using pre-Qin archaic manuscripts) viewed Confucius as a philosopher-sage rather than a prophet. Political motivations drove which school held authority at different periods, and the controversy persisted from the Han dynasty through the twentieth century. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The Burning of Books (213 BC) under Emperor Qin Shi Huang destroyed many I Ching texts that espoused Confucianist philosophy, though the emperor allowed texts on divination to remain — leaving an important gap in the historical record. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

## Modern Western Approaches

### Jesuit Missionaries and First Western Contact

By the late sixteenth century, the I Ching had arrived in Europe via Jesuit missionaries. Jesuit priest **Matteo Ricci (1552–1610)** proposed that the "Tao that produced the myriad things" found in Taoist and Confucian cosmogony was the same as the Abrahamic God, and that the ancient Chinese worshipped a monotheistic God. The Kangxi emperor commissioned French Jesuit priest **Joachim Bouvet (1656–1730)** to compile an annotated translation of the I Ching, though the Vatican ultimately condemned aligning the "pagan" text with Christian values. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Early Western reception was divided: some saw it as philosophy, some as witchcraft, some as evidence of pre-Christian monotheism. Early Jesuit scholars who concerned themselves with the Book of Changes "were all later declared to be insane or heretic." *(Source: 10_wilhelm_iching.md)*

**James Legge (1815–1897)** and **Richard Wilhelm (1873–1930)** produced the two most widely influential Western translations. Legge described the divinatory uses as "vain" and "absurd," but his translation made ancient texts accessible to the Western world and established a biblical exegetical tradition for reading the I Ching. Wilhelm, in contrast, expressed sincere love for China's people and culture. He was influenced by his friendship with Carl Jung and interpreted the Oracle's power as coming from a connection between the human unconscious and the cosmic order: "the manipulation of the yarrow stalks makes it possible for the unconscious in man to become active." His primary translation work was done in collaboration with Chinese scholar Lao Naixuan. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Hellmut Wilhelm (Richard's son) noted that the I Ching had a uniquely democratic function in Chinese culture: "until very recently the Chinese turned to the Book of Changes whenever problems arose in the conduct of life. At temple fairs and at the weekly markets were special stalls where one could obtain oracles. At street corners, soothsayers skilled in the oracle had their permanent tables." *(Source: 10_wilhelm_iching.md)*

### Carl Jung and Synchronicity

Carl Gustav Jung applied the I Ching to expound his theories of synchronicity and archetypal psychology. In his foreword to Wilhelm's translation, Jung stated that the I Ching challenges the Western scientific principle of causality with its alternative model: synchronicity — "a phenomenon of nonlogical and nonscientific events yielding meaning and value in human life." Synchronicity postulates acausal connections between what happens in a divinatory experience and what happens in the physical world. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Jung noted: "While the Western mind carefully sifts, weighs, selects, classifies, isolates, the Chinese picture of the moment encompasses everything down to the minutest nonsensical detail, because all of the ingredients make up the observed moment." For Jung, the unconscious mind was the operative element: "If the I Ching is not accepted by the conscious, at least the unconscious meets it halfway."

Jung maintained that the foundational structure of the I Ching as eight trigrams building upon each other to form sixty-four hexagram arrangements is in line with his archetypal theory. He acknowledged the Book of Changes as one of the most significant contributions to his study of archetypal theory and the unconscious. In his framework, an I Ching reading functions like a clock: just as the ticking hands of a clock represent the present moment in time, the Oracle contains an algorithmic wisdom able to forecast what is to come. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Crucially, Chinese scholars from at least the third century AD had reached similar conclusions. Wang Bi theorized that the yarrow stalks "respond to the questioner as echoes of the spirits governing that change." Shao Yong (AD 1011–1077) concluded that human experiences can be reduced to mathematical ratios and archetypes, arriving at conclusions nine hundred years before Jung about the relationship between the collective consciousness and the Oracle. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

### Western Occultism

Before Jung, the I Ching had already been absorbed into Western spiritualist and ceremonial magic movements. Eliphas Levi (1854–1856) drew equivalences between "the trigrams of Fohi" (Fu Xi) and Western occultism. Aleister Crowley regularly consulted the Oracle and described it as "in some ways the most perfect hieroglyph ever constructed... One may resolve the most obscure spiritual difficulties no less than the most mundane dilemmas." Crowley performed correspondence work between the I Ching trigrams and the Qabalistic Tree of Life. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

Groundbreaking integrative studies comparing the sixty-four hexagrams to the sixty-four triplets of paired codons in DNA continue to be explored in contemporary science. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

## Vietnamese I Ching Tradition [vi]

Vietnam has a long and distinguished engagement with the I Ching. The Book was absorbed into Vietnamese intellectual life around the tenth century, when Confucian classics gained favor. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

**Key historical developments:**

- **Lý dynasty (AD 1009–1225):** I Ching diviners were appointed to court positions to advise the reigning monarchs on statecraft, elevating the Book to a place of prominence among the ruling class.
- **Hậu Lê dynasty (fifteenth century):** Confucianism gained a strong foothold, further popularizing the I Ching.
- **Distinctive Vietnamese synthesis:** While Neo-Confucianism in China was antagonistic toward Taoism and Buddhism, Vietnamese scholars advanced scholarship at the intersection of Yì Xué and Buddhism, and also contributed greatly to the I Ching's magical applications in Taoist mysticism. The Qiang (羌) peoples, ancestors of the Tibetan and Burmese, promulgated I Ching cosmology throughout Southeast Asia, where it was assimilated with animism and the Indigenous folk religions of Vietnam, Thailand, the Malay Kingdom, and Java.
- **Lê Quý Đôn (Lí Guì Dūn, 1726–1784):** The eighteenth-century Vietnamese statesman, philosopher, and scholar — a prodigy said to have mastered the Five Classics by age fourteen — wrote seminal commentaries on the I Ching, the Tao Te Ching, and the Buddhist Diamond Sutra.
- **Nguyễn dynasty (1802–1945):** Robust I Ching scholarship thrived in Vietnam during this period.
- **Caodaism:** An Indigenous Vietnamese religion syncretizing Buddhism, Confucianism, and Taoism, emerging during the Trần dynasty (AD 1225–1400). I Ching metaphysics heavily influenced the precursor mysticism in Caodaism. I Ching divination became part of local mediumship practices, revealing prophecies from the spirit realm.
- **Gua Qi in Vietnam:** Gua Qi approaches to the I Ching are practiced in Vietnam, with varying schools branching off from the historical tenets of Taoist alchemy and fortune-telling.
- **Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries:** Vietnamese scholars developed new schools of Yì Xué commentary that were critical of Zhu Xi and Song dynasty Originalists.

*(Sources: 09_wen_iching.md)*

**The Vietnamese scholarly tradition (Thu Giang Nguyễn Duy Cần, 1907–1998):**

The major Vietnamese I Ching commentary tradition is embodied in the three-volume work of Thu Giang Nguyễn Duy Cần, specifically the *Dịch Kinh Tường Giải* (Detailed Commentary on the Book of Changes), part of a trilogy also including *Dịch Học Tinh Hoa* (Essence of I Ching Studies) and *Chu Dịch Huyền Giải* (Mysterious Commentary on the Zhou I). Written in 1986 and published posthumously in 2014 by NXB Trẻ, the *Dịch Kinh Tường Giải* covers all sixty-four hexagrams using the approach of metaphysical/higher study (Hình nhi thượng học).

The Vietnamese tradition emphasizes that the I Ching should not be reduced to fortune-telling or superstition. In Vietnamese intellectual culture, the Book of Changes is classified within the "Ngũ kinh" (Five Classics, including Kinh Thư, Kinh Thi, Kinh Lễ, Kinh Dịch, and Kinh Xuân Thu) and used as core curriculum in classical academies (viện hàn lâm) during the feudal period.

The Vietnamese commentarial tradition stresses the difficulty of the text: the I Ching uses Tượng (images) and Số (numbers) to express thought, with language serving only as a supplement to clarify these. Even the supplementary language (Thoán từ, Hào từ, Thoán truyện) is "tối cổ" — an extremely archaic form of classical Chinese that almost no one today can read directly from the original. The Vietnamese tradition advises that to truly understand the I Ching requires sustained, non-shallow engagement and the willingness to embrace contradiction as a path to truth.

The Vietnamese tradition also recognizes the I Ching as the foundation of both Lão (Taoist) and Nho (Confucian) thought — collectively known as "Tam Huyền" (Three Mysteries), comprising the I Ching, Lão Tử, and Trang Tử.

*(Source: 01_viet_iching_vol1.md)*

## The Role of Change (Yi, 易)

The word "易" (Yì) holds multiple layers of meaning: it means "to change" or "interchange" — to cause a turn of events in succession — and also "to bestow," "to reveal," and "to exchange hands from one to the other." Change in the context of the I Ching holds a dual nature: it denotes the changes that take place in the natural course of events, and simultaneously denotes the changes that will go into effect as a direct result of having divined on the matter. The Oracle is as much a book of exchanges as it is a book of change. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The foundational principle of the I Ching is that everything is in a continuous process of change — rising and falling in progressive evolutionary advancement. This is the Tao of I (Tao of Change). Although this is the main theme, it is never stated explicitly in the text; it is revealed between the lines and embodied in the succession of the names of the hexagrams and their sequences. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The Tao of I discloses that when situations proceed beyond their extremes, they alternate to their opposites. This is simultaneously a philosophical truth and a practical reminder: in a favorable time and situation, never neglect the unfavorable potential; in an unfavorable time and situation, never act abruptly and blindly; in adverse circumstances, never become depressed and despair. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The ancient sages observed astronomical phenomena in the sky and topographical features on the earth, and studied the relationships among all beings. They realized that in Heaven and earth exists a universal principle: change is absolute and certain; only the principle of change never changes. The I Ching is a record of their experiential knowledge of how to respond and adapt to change in the most suitable way. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The philosophical concept that undergirds the entire work is the unity of Heaven and humanity (天人合一, tiān rén hé yī). The Chinese believe that the Truth of Heaven is also the Truth of humanity. This merging of Heaven and humanity into an organic whole is the foundation of traditional Chinese culture and the source of all spheres of learning and thought throughout Chinese history. The earliest, most systematic book approaching this unity is the I Ching. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

The concept of change also functioned as a corrective force in Chinese history. Hellmut Wilhelm observed that China's great reformers "almost without exception have drawn their inspiration from this book. Since it was a classic, they found in it authoritative backing which helped to smooth the path for their reforms." The concept of change "again and again counteracted a tendency toward permanent ossification of institutions." *(Source: 10_wilhelm_iching.md)*

## Using the I Ching in Practice

### The Purpose of Consultation

The original and enduring purpose of consulting the I Ching is to resolve doubt and confusion. An ancient record says: "When people have doubts about great matters, they consult the tortoise shell and the yarrow stalks." The Chinese have never consulted the I Ching lightly. The guidance offered by the I Ching is based on comprehensive observations of natural laws by ancient sages and their profound experiences of the principle of cause and effect. One still retains free choice. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

When one already knows what one should do according to common sense and moral principles, one should not consult the I Ching. Divine only for important questions and events, never for mean purposes or with selfish motivations. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

### Approaching the Oracle

The I Ching is a book that speaks in images, not words. The ancient Chinese language was composed of pictographs — pictures that do not connect in the same way English speakers think of words as doing. The beauty of this language, and of the I Ching, is that it presents pictures and lets the reader's own imagination resonate with the scene. Once readers understand that reading the I Ching does not mean reading sentences that make sense but rather creating their own personal understanding from archetypal, poetic images, they will come to value it as a great open-ended storehouse of ancient wisdom. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

In every divination, ask only one question. The question should be simple and clear. Avoid vague and optional questions. It is better for a beginner not to ask for a prediction; the best inquiry is for advice. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

### Ritual Preparation

If one's intention is to divine, the I Ching should be used exclusively for divination. When not in use, the book and the yarrow stalks should be wrapped in silk or cloth. Both should be placed on a shelf at about eye level or somewhere clean and significant. Always wash the hands before divination. Ideally face north during divination, with the divine spirit in the most revered position. Three sticks of incense are traditionally lit. The yarrow stalk method induces a deeper level of awareness through its repetitive, meditative process; "the divine and the diviner become closely connected." *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

### Understanding a Reading

The Decision (King Wen's text on the hexagram as a whole) reveals the overall situation. Confucius said: "The wise person, just by contemplating the decision of the gua, is capable of realizing most of the content." Many people, when consulting the I Ching, pay attention to the moving lines while neglecting the overall situation — thereby seeing the trees but missing the forest. The full situation must be understood first. The six lines indicate the six stages of change within that situation. *(Source: 05_alfred_huang_iching.md)*

Reading an I Ching divination is similar to dream interpretation: the content of the Oracle's message can reveal latent issues. What a reading means to you can be profoundly revelatory of what is going on in your subconscious mind. The effort to interpret the Oracle's symbolic lines and metaphors is essentially a form of free association. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

For the Originalist school (Zhu Xi): divination is not a superstitious act of seeking guidance from a supernatural power. Rather, it is an enriching experience of encountering the unknown and unfathomable. In the process of divination, one faces the multiple forces that shape human life, and thereby becomes aware of the opportunities and resources for improving one's situation. The purpose of divination is to walk the path of self-knowledge. Answers are naturally received, not logically deduced. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The I Ching insists upon self-knowledge. Intrinsic to the Book is the encouragement that you can override any negative circumstance you are dealt. When you read the text as literature, you deepen your knowledge of the world. When you divine with the text, you excavate knowledge of yourself and are empowered. When you study the text with discipline and reason, you become the sage. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*

The Book of Changes is both a compass and an atlas for finding your path. Just as a path is found by walking it, to know the Tao, you practice the Way. *(Source: 09_wen_iching.md)*
