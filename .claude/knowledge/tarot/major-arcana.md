# Tarot — Major Arcana (Marseille Tradition)
<!-- sources: 08_marseille_tarot.md -->
<!-- domain: tarot -->

## Overview

The Major Arcana (called the "major suit" in the Marseille tradition) consists of 22 cards with elaborate illustrations, numbered consecutively and each having a specific name. Two cards are exceptional: the Fool has no number, and Card 13 has no name. Numbers are written in Roman numerals in the long notation style (e.g., VIIII not IX). The Marseille numbering places Justice as VIII and Force as XI, which differs from English-school decks that swap these.

The source deck referenced throughout is the CBD Tarot de Marseille, a restoration of the Nicolas Conver 1760 deck by Yoav Ben-Dov.

---

## The Fool's Journey (Suit Sequence)

The major suit sequence can be read as a dynamic story of personal evolution — "the Fool's journey." The numbered cards represent consecutive stages; the Fool (unnumbered) is the traveler walking through them all, finally arriving at the World. The suit moves from earthly social figures at the beginning (low numbers) to celestial, nude, and dreamlike figures toward the end (high numbers), suggesting an ascent from mundane to spiritual reality.

## Card-by-Card Descriptions

---

### 0 (no number) — The Fool
**French title:** Le Mat (or Le Fou)

**Visual description:** A wayfarer or vagabond in a fanciful outfit, richly decorated above but shabby and torn below, walking forward toward the right. He carries a rod over his shoulder with a sack at the end. An animal (dog or cat) scratches at his exposed buttock from the left. His gaze is directed upward rather than at the road ahead.

**Core symbolism:** The three sections of the rod may represent past, present, and future. The number band at top is empty, emphasizing his unclassifiable, exceptional status. The separated sections of the figure suggest each part acting on its own — legs, pelvis, chest, and head — with no coordinated direction.

**Upright meanings:**
- Freedom from conventions, norms, and accepted social structures
- Total openness: giving up control, embracing spontaneity
- Something or someone unique, exceptional, impossible to classify
- Living here and now, without planning for past or future
- A period of aimless roaming without a clear goal; a long journey
- Adaptability; a person who expresses different aspects of personality in different situations
- Uncertainty; an open future not yet determined
- "Fool's luck" — an optimistic feeling of avoiding misfortune along the way

**Reversed meanings:**
- Difficulty in choosing and committing to something stable
- Restlessness, lack of purpose, getting lost
- Foolish behavior, eccentricity
- Lack of acceptance by the social environment
- Difficulty planning ahead

**Key themes / keywords:** Freedom, spontaneity, the present moment, the wanderer, the joker, adaptability, chaos, non-commitment, the Daoist/Zen ideal of emptiness

**Marseille-specific notes:** In the Tarot de Marseille the Fool is exceptional because it carries no number, unlike the Golden Dawn system which assigns it the number 0. This is what makes it an ideal symbol of the traveler rather than the goal of the journey. The French word *mat* means "dull, not shiny"; *le fou* means "the insane." The lower end of his staff may be stuck in the ground — suggesting he goes in circles despite the appearance of forward motion.

### I — The Magician
**French title:** Le Bateleur

**Visual description:** A young man in fancy, colorful garb stands at a table displaying a collection of objects including a cup, a knife, circular coins, and a strange bag. His right hand is raised (holding a wand and another object). His feet point in opposite directions — the left foot enclosed, the right foot stepping onto an open white surface. His hat has an upward-opening brim.

**Core symbolism:** *Bateleur* is an archaic French word for a street conjurer, juggler, or popular showman. The objects on the table recall the four suit symbols. The elongated form between his legs may represent a gateway to a magical reality or the birth canal of new beginnings.

**Upright meanings:**
- A new beginning, the start of a journey or initiative
- Tools and means at one's disposal; acquisition of practical skills
- Personal charm, charisma, persuasion; a showman or public performer
- Use of supernatural forces; creating reality with mind power
- Improvisation; using existing means in creative ways
- The gatekeeper between ordinary reality and the magical realm

**Reversed meanings:**
- Trickery, sleight of hand, cheating; a charlatan or con artist
- Showing off, pretending; near miss due to inexperience or inaccuracy
- Lack of self-awareness about body, sexuality, or basic motives
- Hidden inner confusion under a confident exterior

**Key themes / keywords:** Beginnings, tools, magic, illusion, individuality, performance, starting point

**Marseille-specific notes:** The table extends outside the card frame, and the bag may contain unseen tools — hinting at untapped potentials. The feet pointing in opposite directions suggest hesitation beneath the confident surface. The wand in one hand and a feminine-shaped object in the other may represent masculine and feminine symbols he tries but fails to fully unite.

### II — The Popess
**French title:** La Papesse

**Visual description:** A heavily veiled and robed female figure seated on a throne. She holds an open book, partly hidden by her robe. She is turned to the left. Her tiara is taller than in other court cards, with the title strip exceptionally narrow at the top. A curtain or screen rises behind her.

**Core symbolism:** Refers to the legend of Pope Joan (Johanna), a woman who disguised herself as a man and rose to become Pope before her secret was revealed. The book, the screen behind her, and the layered coverings all emphasize knowledge, mystery, and concealment.

**Upright meanings:**
- Ancient wisdom combining intellect and intuition, especially feminine knowledge
- A spiritual mother; a wise guide or teacher
- Secrets, something hidden, mystery; things guarded behind a veil
- Modesty; a person who does not boast about their qualities
- Setting firm boundaries and preserving personal identity
- Knowing how to keep one's counsel; prudent non-action in practical matters
- May represent the reader themselves, or indicate the querent is concealing something

**Reversed meanings:**
- A need to hide one's true nature behind social conventions
- Conservative approach to the body and sexuality
- Emotional blockage; excessive secrecy

**Key themes / keywords:** Wisdom, mystery, the hidden, boundaries, the spiritual feminine, secrets, intuition, ancient knowledge

**Marseille-specific notes:** In English-school decks this card is called "the High Priestess." The Marseille Popess is notably more covered than any other figure in the major suit — chest, pelvis, and legs are all concealed, expressing the dominance of intellect and the suppression of desire and feeling. The card may appear as a last card when the reading's answer is still hidden "behind the veil."

### III — The Empress
**French title:** L'Imperatrice

**Visual description:** A crowned female figure seated on a throne, embracing a heraldic eagle shield and holding a large scepter that slants from her pelvis toward the right. Her lower body is wide and rounded. The back of the seat is soft and unfinished, with flowing lines on the left and straight lines on the right.

**Core symbolism:** The eagle is a symbol of royal European families and legitimacy. The large scepter is a phallic symbol of power and creative force. The number three and the triangular composition suggest fertility and growth.

**Upright meanings:**
- Abundance, growth, productivity, natural evolution bearing fruit
- Mother earth; fertility and natural forces of life
- A warm, protective mother figure or strong female personality
- Emotional intelligence; acting from the gut with passion
- A touch of warmth within an institutional framework
- Positive developments; advancement; a feeling of plenty

**Reversed meanings:**
- Impulsive behavior; someone difficult to reason with
- Overprotectiveness; excessive involvement in others' lives
- Problems with a dominant or overwhelming mother figure

**Key themes / keywords:** Fertility, growth, motherhood, abundance, feminine power, earth, natural force

**Marseille-specific notes:** If the Popess conceals her femininity, the Empress openly displays it as a source of power. The Empress combines natural, flowing lines with artificial, structured ones — expressing organic fertility within a social framework. An Adam's apple trace and a phallic scepter from the abdomen hint that the Empress does not shy from unfeminine expressions of authority when needed.

### IIII — The Emperor
**French title:** L'Empereur

**Visual description:** An armored male figure seated on a throne with crossed legs and a military helmet-shaped crown. He holds a combined scepter-orb with a cross in his right hand and his left hand grips his belt. A heraldic eagle appears on the shield. His gaze is directed to the left.

**Core symbolism:** The helmet-crown represents warrior nobility. The orb-cross symbolizes earthly rule subordinate to spiritual/Christian law. Crossed legs indicate a king sitting in judgment (requiring self-discipline and containment).

**Upright meanings:**
- Authority and domination in the material world; governance, military, management
- A strong, assertive personality; leadership and self-confidence
- An earthly father figure; a patron or protector
- Conservative values of honor, virtue, and discipline
- Determination and willingness to fight and protect one's ground
- A stable material status or a rich person

**Reversed meanings:**
- Belligerence, violence, dictatorship; solving things by brute force
- A tyrannical personality or constant need to control
- Possibility of sexual abuse; macho attitude
- Denial and hiding of inner weaknesses behind toughness

**Key themes / keywords:** Authority, material power, father, leadership, control, stability, warfare, domination

**Marseille-specific notes:** In contrast to the Empress who embraces her symbols softly, the Emperor holds them at rigid distance. The shield may be positioned over a hole in the ground — hinting at an abyss of hidden fears beneath the powerful surface. His gaze to the left suggests conservatism: he relies on past achievements and ancestral authority rather than forward movement.

### V — The Pope
**French title:** Le Pape

**Visual description:** A seated figure in papal vestments with a triple-barred cross and a triple tiara (three crowns). His right hand forms the Latin gesture of benediction with two straight fingers. Two small tonsured figures in front of him look away, their backs to us; the Pope seems to favor one over the other.

**Core symbolism:** *Pape* comes from "father." The triple tiara represents the church's dominion on earth. The benediction gesture points upward, marking a diagonal line of ascent. The two disciples receive unequal attention.

**Upright meanings:**
- A teacher, instructor, counselor, or spiritual guide
- Institutional knowledge; schools, universities, hospitals, courts
- Advice from a certified professional expert
- Spiritual fatherhood; a guru or religious leader
- Official procedures of status recognition (marriage or divorce)
- Moral conscience; values absorbed in childhood
- Conventional and institutional therapy or medicine

**Reversed meanings:**
- Excessive adhesion to outdated conventions and norms
- Bureaucracy; an oppressive establishment
- Hypocrisy, discrimination, prejudice
- Unfounded claims made in the name of religion or science
- Divorce (as the inverse of marriage)

**Key themes / keywords:** Guidance, knowledge, institution, spiritual authority, teaching, tradition, conformity, fatherhood

**Marseille-specific notes:** The Golden Dawn renamed this card "the Hierophant" to remove the Catholic reference. A possible hidden knife under the raised hand of the preferred disciple hints at betrayal by the favored one. The flesh-colored gloves suggest that the person and the institution are inseparable.

### VI — The Lover
**French title:** L'Amoureux

**Visual description:** A young man stands between two women — a younger one on his right and an older one on his left. His body leans toward the young woman but his head turns toward the older one. A Cupid or angel in the sky aims an arrow at the young woman from a white circle. All three figures have their arms entwined.

**Core symbolism:** The three figures may reference the mythological story of Hercules at a crossroads choosing between the path of pleasure (young woman) and the path of virtue (older woman). The inverted Y shape of the man's body symbolizes Pythagorean youth's crossroads. The Cupid's arrow suggests both romantic love and a divine hint or sign.

**Upright meanings:**
- Love; a romantic relationship or falling in love
- Standing at a crossroads; a meaningful choice with long-term consequences
- A complex romantic situation involving multiple people
- Small, real steps taken in one direction reveal the heart's true orientation
- The will of heaven aligns with the inclinations of the heart
- A decision whose consequences will accompany one for the rest of life

**Reversed meanings:**
- A romantic triangle or tension between mother and romantic partner
- Hesitation, quandary; confusion about one's own feelings
- Difficulty freeing oneself from past influences or old habits

**Key themes / keywords:** Love, choice, crossroads, youth, desire vs. wisdom, the heart, a fork in the road, romantic entanglement

**Marseille-specific notes:** The French school called this card "the Two Paths," emphasizing choice over love. The English school emphasized the romantic meaning. In the Marseille tradition, the body leaning toward the young woman and the head toward the older one encodes a conflict between desire and reason. The hand on the young woman's belly may hint at future pregnancy — a creation depending on the lover's choice.

### VII — The Chariot
**French title:** Le Chariot

**Visual description:** An armored warrior rides a chariot, wearing a crown and carrying a spear. The lower half of the chariot is asymmetrical and dreamlike, appearing to float. Two horse-like creatures (or face masks on the shoulders) pull the chariot. The card number appears on the front of the chariot. The letters V.T appear on the front plate.

**Core symbolism:** Likely references medieval depictions of Alexander the Great carried skyward by gryphons. The square chariot body and triangular shape of the rider's body capture the tension between stability (four) and movement (three) inherent in the number seven.

**Upright meanings:**
- Victory or achievement placing the querent in a strong, secure position
- Ambition, energy, motivation to move forward; a march of triumph
- Public honor; a prestigious status symbol
- Aspiration to high social position
- Advance toward a desired goal

**Reversed meanings:**
- Inner weakness hidden behind external show
- Arrogance, vanity, overconfidence
- Emotional closure; overprotectiveness
- Confusion about goals; the horse pulling toward old habits without deliberate direction
- Dependence on external symbols of success rather than inner resources

**Key themes / keywords:** Victory, triumph, movement, status, ambition, protection, controlled advance, momentum

**Marseille-specific notes:** The rider holds no reins — suggesting the horse (habit, desire) drives the direction, not the conscious will. The lower part of the chariot has no solid ground on the right side. The Alexander story warns that ambition to conquer the sky leads to hubris.

### VIII — Justice
**French title:** Justice

**Visual description:** A crowned figure seated on a throne in a formal, upright posture. In her right hand (card's left side) she holds a sword pointing upward; in her left hand (card's right side) she holds scales. Her elbow touches the scales, tilting them slightly to the right. Straight lines and right angles dominate the upper part of the card; wavy earth and soft clothing appear at the bottom.

**Core symbolism:** Derived from Maat (Egyptian goddess) and Archangel Michael at the Last Judgment. The scales weigh pros and cons; the sword both punishes and decides. The elbow actively tipping the scales introduces a human element of grace beyond strict law.

**Upright meanings:**
- Law and order; legal and court procedures
- A fair, balanced judgment; rational deliberation
- A developed conscience and moral standards
- Clear, rational thinking; acting by accepted norms
- A decisive cut after long deliberation
- A touch of grace tipping the scales favorably

**Reversed meanings:**
- Petty accountability; a critical and judgmental attitude
- Guilt feelings; self-mortification; repressive self-control
- Negative, blocking ideas that prevent change and advance
- Crooked or unfair judgment

**Key themes / keywords:** Law, balance, fairness, rationality, judgment, precision, moral standards, the sword of decision

**Marseille-specific notes:** In the Marseille tradition Justice is card VIII and Force is card XI — the reverse of English-school decks, which switched them for Cabbalistic reasons. The Marseille placement is traditional and considered authentic. *Juste* in French means both "just" and "precise."

### VIIII — The Hermit
**French title:** L'Hermite

**Visual description:** A bearded, robed figure in a long mantle and hood, walking slowly to the left while holding a lamp or lantern shielded by a sleeve of his robe. His stance is concentrated and focused; the fingers of his extended hand create a sharp, pointed shape. Three horizontal lines mark his forehead.

**Core symbolism:** References Diogenes walking with a lit candle in daylight, searching for a true human. The lamp illuminates only a small area ahead; the mantle sleeve keeps the light close. The three forehead lines echo the Hindu *vibhuti* symbol of the "third eye."

**Upright meanings:**
- A quest for truth or spiritual understanding
- Concentrating on a clear purpose; determination toward a goal
- Caution, meticulous examination before any move; embodiment of Prudence (the missing fourth cardinal virtue)
- Self-deprivation and asceticism for the sake of a meaningful cause
- Turning inward; a period of self-examination and inactivity
- Independence of thought; not caring about others' opinions

**Reversed meanings:**
- A closed, reclusive attitude; isolation and loneliness
- Excessive caution and suspicion; fear of intimacy
- Fixed ideas; ideological extremism or fanaticism
- Overcritical search for faults in others; searching for an ideal partner as an excuse to avoid real contact
- Hidden and denied desires beneath a puritan exterior

**Key themes / keywords:** Solitude, truth-seeking, wisdom, inner quest, caution, self-denial, focus, contemplation, the spiritual seeker

**Marseille-specific notes:** The mantle sleeve around the lamp creates an image of keeping one's light private — a double meaning of careful illumination and hoarding of insight. The odd curving stick in the Hermit's hand may allude to the suit of wands and hidden, crooked desires beneath the ascetic surface.

### X — The Wheel of Fortune
**French title:** La Roue de Fortune

**Visual description:** A large wheel on an unstable-looking mechanical device stands over wavy ground lines. On the wheel's sides are two creatures (one descending, one ascending) with animal-human characteristics. At the top sits a crowned sphinx-like creature with a sword. No hand turns the wheel; it moves by its own momentum.

**Core symbolism:** References Fortuna, the Roman goddess of luck, and medieval depictions of the wheel showing figures labeled "I shall reign," "I reign," "I reigned," "I have no reign." The sphinx holds all four attributes of the World card's living creatures.

**Upright meanings:**
- Change in circumstances and position; life cycles
- A rise after a fall; consolation that descents are followed by ascents
- The right side of the wheel is rising; the left side is falling — the card on the right in a spread indicates a rising factor
- Gambling; putting faith in capricious luck
- Adapting to the routine of everyday life; biorhythmic cycles
- Closure of circles; returning to a starting point; past incarnations

**Reversed meanings:**
- Decline after a period of ascent; danger at the summit
- Moving in a closed circle without real progress
- Capricious mood changes; feeling powerless to change one's situation
- Mechanisms of a bureaucratic system losing their basis

**Key themes / keywords:** Fortune, cycles, change, ups and downs, luck, the turning wheel, samsara, revolution, fate

**Marseille-specific notes:** The wheel's ground lines look like water waves and its axis is partly missing — suggesting nothing is certain or permanent. The sphinx at the top may attack whoever reaches the highest position, not simply stay there. The Buddhist image of samsara with three animals at the center parallels the three creatures on the wheel.

### XI — Force
**French title:** Force

**Visual description:** A woman in a wide-brimmed hat (with six spikes) tames a lion whose head grows from her pelvis area. She touches the lion's mouth delicately with her fingertips — it is unclear whether she is opening or closing it. The lion gazes upward. A line on the neck separates the woman's head from her body. Her chest is tightly laced. Very little ground appears at the bottom right.

**Core symbolism:** May reference Cyrene (Greek nymph who fought a lion), the Hindu goddess Durga/Kali, and Samson's or Hercules's battles with lions. The lion growing from the pelvis links it to the suit of wands and sexual/creative desires.

**Upright meanings:**
- Strength and domination through gentleness and cooperation, not violence
- Self-confidence without the need for forceful oppression
- Self-control; the intellect (head) taming animal desires (lion/pelvis)
- Courage and daring to face challenges; taking calculated risks
- Collaborating rather than fighting; taming rather than suppressing

**Reversed meanings:**
- Constant tensions from needing to keep everything under control
- Risk of losing one's grip; internal conflict
- Unrealistic assessment of one's forces may lead to failure
- Loss of touch with feelings and passions due to excessive intellectual detachment

**Key themes / keywords:** Inner strength, self-mastery, courage, gentleness, taming desire, collaboration, controlled passion

**Marseille-specific notes:** In Marseille tradition Force is card XI (Justice is VIII). English-school decks reversed these numbers, renaming it "Strength." The six spikes on the woman's hat echo the six teeth in the lion's mouth — suggesting she integrates the fierce passions rather than merely suppressing them. The abyss at the bottom right suggests animal passions originate in depths beyond rational control.

### XII — The Hanged Man
**French title:** Le Pendu

**Visual description:** A man hangs upside down from a wooden crossbar by one leg, with the other leg bent at the knee forming a triangle. His arms are either tied or held behind his back. Twelve red-tipped branch stumps surround the wood frame. No sky or landscape is visible; the only ground appears as green areas on the card sides.

**Core symbolism:** References crucified Christians, Judas Iscariot, and Odin's self-hanging on Yggdrasil to discover runic wisdom. The twelve branch tips may symbolize a complete range of possibilities (like the zodiac), all cut off. The only opening is downward through the hole in the ground.

**Upright meanings:**
- Seeing things from a unique, nonconformist point of view
- Enduring difficulties for a worthy cause; acceptance of sacrifice
- A period of deep self-examination and inactivity
- Passive acceptance of whatever comes; surrender to reality as it is
- Putting everything in question, unlike the Hermit who accepts existing beliefs
- The preparatory trial before the transforming experiences that follow

**Reversed meanings:**
- Isolation; emotional stance of a victim
- Inability to act; feeling completely helpless
- Denying one's unique qualities; striving to be "normal" at all costs
- Living in a private, imaginary reality

**Key themes / keywords:** Suspension, sacrifice, surrender, unique perspective, self-examination, passive acceptance, the upside-down view, initiation

**Marseille-specific notes:** The card has a similarity to the central figure of the World (card XXI) in an inverted position. When the Hanged Man is straightened (inverse card in a reading), it can represent a futile attempt to appear "normal" — while the upright position represents honest acceptance of being different. The only way forward is downward through the abyss.

### XIII — (No name)
**French title:** (none)

**Visual description:** A large skeleton wielding a scythe moves to the right over a dark, largely black field. Under the scythe's blade lie scattered body parts — limbs and two heads, one crowned (male/royal) and one uncrowned (female). The skeleton's spine base is colored red. The ground above the blade is tinged with yellow. The card's blackness is the darkest in the entire major suit.

**Core symbolism:** The skeleton is the grim reaper. The missing name emphasizes things too frightening to name. The scythe curves upward toward the right — the direction of the future. The crowned head represents past authority figures now trampled. In English-school decks this card is called "Death."

**Upright meanings:**
- The end of something whose time has come; abrupt termination, not gradual decline
- Cutting off past influences or emotional dependence on authority figures
- Giving up the superfluous and keeping only the essential
- A shock or crisis that exposes the true nature of things
- Disintegration of the old clears ground for the new; a new beginning follows
- A mature perspective that keeps death in view as an advisor

**Reversed meanings:**
- Difficulty coping with loss or change; painful realization
- Temporary difficulties; a trying challenge
- Does not predict literal future death; may reflect grief or anxiety about dying

**Key themes / keywords:** Transformation, endings, the essential, change, irreversible cuts, clearing away the old, the skeleton, the harvest

**Marseille-specific notes:** The Marseille tradition deliberately left this card unnamed — unlike the English school that gave it the title "Death." If this card appears, the author recommends addressing it immediately with the querent to clarify it is about change, not literal death. The scythe curves upward and the skeleton moves right — indicating the future. The disintegration is already complete; the skeleton cuts what is already on the ground.

### XIIII — Temperance
**French title:** Temperance

**Visual description:** A winged angelic figure with a flower on its forehead pours liquid between two vessels in an apparently impossible, gravity-defying arc. The lower half of the body leans right, the upper half leans left. The dress combines red and blue. The figure is firmly grounded but has wings. No additional figures or landscape appear.

**Core symbolism:** *Temperance* from Latin means "pouring or mixing liquids to obtain an average blend" (originally: diluting wine with water). One of the four Christian cardinal virtues. The miracle of Cana. The liquid poured before the abdomen suggests an internal, alchemical flow.

**Upright meanings:**
- Reconciliation, compromise, finding the middle road
- Integration of opposites; a mediator between conflicting parties
- Ability to do the seemingly impossible with precision and skill
- A slow, patient process of self-distillation and improvement
- Patience, perseverance; a slow pace of events
- Alternative medicine; holistic self-balance; yoga, tai chi, Reiki

**Reversed meanings:**
- Going back and forth without real progress; endless hesitation
- Losing patience with a lengthy process
- Excessive preoccupation with oneself, pushing away others

**Key themes / keywords:** Balance, moderation, patience, the golden mean, blending opposites, the impossible made possible, inner alchemy, slow refinement

**Marseille-specific notes:** The only major suit card whose French title is written without the definite article ("Temperance" not "La Temperance"). The raised elbows fending off others while doing internal work can indicate a degree of self-absorption that blocks practical advance or close contact with others. Temperance is the gentle reconciliation that follows the dark transformation of Card 13.

### XV — The Devil
**French title:** Le Diable

**Visual description:** A large central figure with animal feet, horns, a male sexual organ, and female breasts stands on a dark black base. Its tongue is stuck out. Two small imp figures are tied at their necks to the central figure's pedestal by ropes. The imps have small horns, tails, and feet resembling roots; their gazes are directed upward toward the central figure's penis.

**Core symbolism:** May reference the Greek god Pan (wild natural passions), a Babylonian Burney relief (~1800 BCE), and Christian depictions of the Devil. The black base is the abyss. The imps' feet resemble tree roots, their horns resemble branches — suggesting a process of growth from darkness.

**Upright meanings:**
- A burst of creativity or passion breaking through conventional limits
- Anarchism, subversion, defiance of norms; forbidden experiences
- Unrestrained expression of desires and sexuality
- Inner paradoxes and contradictions beyond binary logic
- Moving forward after a past family trauma; a process of reparation
- Creative impulse going beyond accepted limits

**Reversed meanings:**
- Temptation; attraction to the dark and forbidden
- Exploitation, egotism, domination; a relationship of abuse
- Compulsive self-gratification; addiction to pleasure
- Difficulty detaching from an unhealthy bond
- Self-defeating behavior that will harm the querent

**Key themes / keywords:** Passion, desire, the forbidden, anarchism, paradox, the shadow self, bondage, creativity, subversion, dark forces

**Marseille-specific notes:** The Marseille devil is smiling and bawdy, not frightening like English-school depictions. The penis and breasts may be artificial accessories — "even though everything is exposed, nothing can be taken at face value." The central figure may represent a baby's face — with the imps as parents still bound to the darkness they came from, while the next generation rises.

### XVI — The Tower
**French title:** La Maison Dieu ("The House of God")

**Visual description:** A tall brick tower is struck by an ambiguous energy source from above (the top right corner of the card). A crown sits on top of the tower. Three windows appear on the tower face. Two clothed figures appear near the base — one on each side. Small colored circles (resembling manna or hailstones) fall through the air around the tower. The figures do not clearly fall; one may be crawling around from behind.

**Core symbolism:** References the Tower of Babel, St. Barbara's imprisonment, and divine fire from heaven. The French title *La Maison Dieu* suggests a temple receiving divine energy rather than simply a tower being destroyed.

**Upright meanings:**
- Breaking up of solid structures; sudden collapse of what appeared stable
- Liberation from confinement; removal of external or internal barriers
- A sudden revelation or insight like a lightning flash
- Sudden breakthrough after long preparations
- A return to humble, solid ground after vain illusions of grandeur
- A passionate sexual encounter or state of ecstasy

**Reversed meanings:**
- Shock; chaos and confusion; difficulty understanding what is happening
- Collapse of trusted structures; a fall from an apparently secure position
- Vanity and pride leading to failure
- Tension accumulating until it explodes

**Key themes / keywords:** Disruption, breakthrough, revelation, collapse, liberation, divine energy, crisis as opportunity, the fall, the sudden change

**Marseille-specific notes:** The title *La Maison Dieu* gives this card a spiritual dimension absent from English-school "Tower" cards. The figures near the base may be gathering manna (colorful circles as heavenly food), not simply falling. The flesh-colored tower may represent the physical body as a living temple. The gravity suspension of the figures hints at a moment when ordinary laws momentarily dissolve.

### XVII — The Star
**French title:** L'Etoile

**Visual description:** A large central star (resembling a compass rose) is surrounded by seven small stars. Below, a nude female figure kneels in an open landscape, pouring liquid from two vessels — one onto the ground (left vessel), one onto herself (right vessel). A bird perches in a tree at the right. The lower body details blend ambiguously with the landscape.

**Core symbolism:** References the alchemical triad of sun/moon/star (masculine principle, feminine principle, their mystical union). The seven small stars may be the Pleiades. The North Star's fixed central position represents a guiding point above the flow of earthly change.

**Upright meanings:**
- Openness, simplicity, and return to nature; "naked as you are"
- Purity, honesty; accepting one's body and desires as they are
- Showing oneself with all weaknesses and flaws — readiness for self-acceptance
- Generosity; giving freely without calculation
- Luck from heaven (*mazal* in Hebrew means both luck and something flowing)
- Intuitive guidance; energy from a higher plane flowing down

**Reversed meanings:**
- Naive optimism; wishful thinking; exposing oneself to danger or abuse
- Difficulty setting proper boundaries
- Squandering, wastefulness; overflowing generosity becoming harmful

**Key themes / keywords:** Openness, purity, honesty, hope, generosity, flowing grace, the natural, vulnerability, celestial guidance

**Marseille-specific notes:** The physical distortions in the woman's lower body are intentional in the Marseille tradition — readiness to accept imperfection rather than idealize. The blending of body and landscape in the lower portion of the card expresses the softening of rigid limits. The bird may be a dove (safety/peace) or a black silhouette suggesting uncertainty and danger nearby.

### XVIII — The Moon
**French title:** La Lune

**Visual description:** A large moon face with downturned expression looks toward the left. Below it, two dogs (or wolves) face each other barking. Between them, a crustacean emerges from a pool. Two towers flank the scene — the left one appears open and lit, the right one closed and shaded. The pool has a textured bottom of raw rocks. Floating drops descend from the moon toward the animals. The atmosphere is lined in light blue, suggesting moonlight.

**Core symbolism:** The moon as the feminine principle in alchemy; the border zone between earthly and celestial domains in medieval cosmology. The crab relates to Cancer (astrological sign of home, womb, mother). The two dogs barking represent a quarrel whose real motives are hidden; the crustacean symbolizes contents rising from the deep.

**Upright meanings:**
- Deep emotions rising from the unconscious; content from the distant past
- A different experience of reality: dreams, altered states, the nocturnal
- Longing for something emotionally unreachable
- Past influences (especially early childhood or the mother) impacting the present
- Finding one's hidden strengths by going to the very bottom
- Hidden dangers or motives beneath the surface

**Reversed meanings:**
- Vague, disturbing feelings; emotional difficulties; depression
- Danger lurking under the surface; retreat
- The road ahead is hard to find; emotional decline

**Key themes / keywords:** The unconscious, past influences, hidden forces, the night, the mother, dreams, deep emotions, the abyss, difficult passage, endurance

**Marseille-specific notes:** The left side of the card (moon face, dominant dog, taller pool edge) is consistently stronger than the right, suggesting dominance of the past. At the bottom of the pool, solid rock offers foundation — "one cannot fall indefinitely." The French expression "the dog barks at the moon" means a futile attempt to oppose something over which you have no influence.

### XVIIII — The Sun
**French title:** Le Soleil

**Visual description:** A large radiant sun fills the upper portion of the card with smooth, straight rays. Below, two children — nearly naked except for loincloths — stand in a partially walled space, touching each other warmly at the shoulders and heart. One child has a remnant of a tail; the other appears more alert and developed. The sun seems almost to touch their heads.

**Core symbolism:** The sun as masculine, fatherly principle (medieval alchemy, astrology, absolute monarchy). The two children may be twins or brothers — possibly the Ashvin twins of Indian mythology, who have healing powers. The low wall expresses boundaries that are present but moderate.

**Upright meanings:**
- Light and warmth; blessings coming from above; abundance and prosperity
- Healing energy; help from people in high positions or from higher forces
- Matters relating to children; childhood; one's inner child
- Partnership, trust, warmth, sharing; finding a "twin soul"
- Setting moderate, nonoppressive limits; a human touch in management
- A positive, optimistic situation; comfort and material ease

**Reversed meanings:**
- Living in a limited, protected space; difficulty facing reality openly
- Immaturity; dependence on others; living in a "fool's paradise"
- An absent or idealized (unreachable) father figure
- A sexual block in a romantic relationship; overly childish partners

**Key themes / keywords:** Warmth, abundance, blessings, children, brotherhood, guidance, healing, measured limits, optimism, paternal light

**Marseille-specific notes:** Jodorowsky interprets the Sun as a father in heaven — an idealized, absent father figure. The tail remnant on one child and the collar rings echo features of the imps in the Devil card — suggesting this may be a more enlightened phase of the same relationship, the "shadow" self being gently led toward light. The white surface under the alert child's feet echoes the Magician, suggesting a new beginning.

### XX — Judgment
**French title:** Le Jugement

**Visual description:** An angel blows a large trumpet in the sky above. A line pattern on the flag or banner resembles an inverted trumpet shape. Below, three naked figures emerge from an opening in the earth: a central figure rising from the hole and two parental figures on each side reaching toward it. The central figure's back has a herringbone pattern. The sky, earth, and an abyss opening are all present and open toward each other.

**Core symbolism:** Based on the Christian Last Judgment/Apocalypse (Revelation of St. John). The cross on the angel's banner may represent the meeting of horizontal (earthly) and vertical (spiritual) axes. The three figures form a triangle with a family configuration. The abyss is now illuminated.

**Upright meanings:**
- Revelation, enlightenment, a new understanding; an awakening
- A significant turning point in a therapeutic process
- Healing of family relationships; redemption from past suffering
- Disclosure of secrets; publicity; matters becoming common knowledge
- Birth of something new; rebirth and personal transformation
- A moment of grace when heaven manifests on earth

**Reversed meanings:**
- Revelation of something that should have remained private
- Lack of privacy; unpleasant realization
- Problems in child-parent relations
- Too much noise and drama; inappropriate disclosure

**Key themes / keywords:** Awakening, revelation, rebirth, the trumpet call, spiritual transformation, the apocalypse, family healing, disclosure, the meeting of axes

**Marseille-specific notes:** Jodorowsky reads the card name as *le juge ment* ("the judge lies"), suggesting every judgment is ultimately false. The tonsured head of the rising figure echoes the Pope's disciples, indicating a long path of spiritual learning. The white areas around the pelvis express purity in sexual matters. The Judgment card completes an inner quest; the World card (XXI) completes the external, practical journey.

### XXI — The World
**French title:** Le Monde

**Visual description:** A nearly nude dancing figure (partially covered by a light scarf) is enclosed within an oblong wreath of leaves. The figure holds a wand in the right hand and a receptacle (possibly womb-shaped) in the left. In each corner of the card stands one of the four living creatures: bull (lower left), lion (lower right), human (upper left), eagle (upper right). The composition is formally symmetrical.

**Core symbolism:** Based on medieval "Christ in Majesty" (mandorla), Hildegard von Bingen's "cosmic man," and the Hindu dancing god Shiva Nataraj. The four living creatures are the biblical tetramorph (Ezekiel, Revelation), representing the four evangelists and the four suits. The central dancer represents consciousness uniting all domains.

**Upright meanings:**
- Completion of a process; balanced achievements across multiple life domains
- Harmony and integration; a perfect vision of things in their proper place
- Realization and success; the positive outcome of a project
- Contact with far places; foreign travel or international connections
- Something new about to be born; pregnancy (literal or metaphorical)
- The dance of life; full personal realization

**Reversed meanings:**
- Life in a bubble; difficulty sharing one's world with others
- Disconnection between inner feelings and external situation
- Narcissism; preoccupation with oneself; the world revolving around you
- Inability to move forward; isolation; a golden cage

**Key themes / keywords:** Completion, harmony, integration, wholeness, the cosmos, the dance of opposites, realization, the embryo, birth, the four elements united

**Marseille-specific notes:** The dancing figure resembles a medieval medical depiction of a human embryo — folded leg, arms spread inside a round womb — suggesting the World is simultaneously the end of one journey and the preparation for the next. The World "gives birth" to the Magician (card I), completing the circle of the Fool's journey. The wand (right/masculine) and receptacle (left/feminine) held in the figure's hands express the polarity driving the cosmic dance.

## The Four Cardinal Virtues in the Major Suit

Three of the four classical cardinal virtues appear as cards in the major suit, at equal intervals:
- **Justice** (VIII)
- **Force** (XI)
- **Temperance** (XIV)

The fourth virtue, **Prudence**, is absent — though some interpreters assign that role to the Hermit (IX).

## Nudity as Symbolic Language

- **Dressed figures** (beginning of suit, cards I–X): Social identity, earthly status, established role
- **Partially or fully nude figures** (latter half, cards XII–XXI): Exposure, vulnerability, openness to higher forces, removal of social markers, spiritual awakening
- The progression from clothed to nude parallels the journey from the mundane world to spiritual realization
