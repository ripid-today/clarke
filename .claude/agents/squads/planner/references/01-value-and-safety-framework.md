# Value and Safety Framework — Planner Reference

Planner's foundation is the work of Benjamin Graham, updated for the Vietnam context. Graham's insight — buy assets at a discount to intrinsic value, and the margin of safety will protect you even when you are wrong — is even more critical in a market with VAS opacity, thin liquidity, and structurally higher accounting risk.

---

## 1. Graham's Margin of Safety — The Core Principle

The margin of safety is the difference between intrinsic value and the price paid. Graham articulated this in "The Intelligent Investor" (revised edition, Ch. 20) as the central concept of sound investment:

"The margin of safety is always dependent on the price paid. It will be large at one price, small at some higher price, nonexistent at some still higher price."

**The operational rules:**

**The 33% Discount Rule (Defensive):** Never pay more than 2/3 of intrinsic value. The remaining 1/3 is the margin of safety — the buffer that protects you if your analysis is wrong, if the company hits a bad year, or if the market remains irrational longer than expected.

**The 50% Discount Rule (For Deep Value):** When possible, buy at 50% of intrinsic value. This applies to net-net stocks, distressed assets, and situations where the intrinsic value is highly reliable (hard assets, liquid securities).

### Why the Margin of Safety Matters in Vietnam

VAS (Vietnamese Accounting Standards) reporting is less rigorous than IFRS:
- Revenue recognition rules are less strict — earnings can be pulled forward
- Related-party transactions are systematically under-disclosed
- Subsidiary consolidation may include entities that are actually off-balance-sheet
- Management guidance has historically been optimistic in Vietnam's bull markets

These opacity factors mean that Planner's calculated intrinsic value may be HIGHER than the true intrinsic value. The margin of safety exists partly to cover this analytical uncertainty.

**Vietnam margin of safety adjustment:** Planner adds 10-15% to the standard Graham discount to account for VAS opacity risk. Where Graham would require a 33% discount, Planner requires a 40-45% discount for Vietnam listed companies. Where Graham requires a 50% discount, Planner requires 55-60%.

## 2. Graham's Defensive Investor Criteria (Chapter 14 Checklist)

These 9 criteria define a safe, defensive investment. A company meeting all 9 passes Graham's quality floor. A company failing 3 or more is speculative. Planner uses this as a baseline screen.

**1. Adequate size (not a small company):**
Original Graham: revenues >$100M (1970s USD). Vietnam equivalent: market capitalization ≥ VND 1,000 billion (roughly $40M USD) and annual revenues ≥ VND 500 billion. Below this threshold, governance and liquidity risk both increase significantly.

**2. Sufficiently strong financial condition:**
Current ratio > 2.0 (current assets are at least twice current liabilities). This ensures the company can meet short-term obligations even under stress.

Long-term debt should not exceed net current assets (current assets minus total current liabilities). This is a very conservative standard — most growth companies will fail it.

**3. Earnings stability:**
Positive earnings in each of the past 10 years. For Vietnam, where many companies were privatized more recently, Planner accepts the full available track record (minimum 5 years of public reporting).

**4. Dividend record:**
Uninterrupted dividend payments for at least 10 years. For Vietnam, where dividend policies are less stable, Planner accepts 5 years of uninterrupted cash dividends.

**5. Earnings growth:**
EPS must have grown at least 33% over the past 10 years (approximately 3% per year — this is an intentionally low bar, designed to exclude companies in secular decline). Calculate: [(EPS in most recent year) ÷ (EPS 10 years ago)] ≥ 1.33.

**6. Moderate P/E:**
P/E ≤ 15× based on the most recent 3-year average earnings. The 3-year average smooths cyclical distortions. In Vietnam's market, which trades at 12-15× on average, a stock at 15× or below is not expensive.

**7. Moderate price-to-book ratio:**
P/B ≤ 1.5×. Book value is less reliable in Vietnam (VAS often overstates assets and understates liabilities), but a P/B above 3× is a red flag without exceptional ROE to justify it.

**8. Combined multiplier check:**
Graham's combined formula: (P/E × P/B) ≤ 22.5. This allows higher P/E when P/B is low and vice versa, but prevents both from being elevated simultaneously.

**9. No excess long-term debt:**
Long-term debt ≤ book value of equity. Many Vietnamese companies, particularly real estate developers and SOEs, carry significant debt — this criterion would flag them as speculative by Graham's standards.

### Using the Checklist

Planner uses the checklist as a scoring tool, not a pass/fail filter in most cases:

- 7-9 criteria met: Fundamentally sound — passes the quality floor
- 5-6 criteria met: Acceptable with noted weaknesses
- 3-4 criteria met: Speculative — risk score starts at minimum 6
- ≤2 criteria met: Highly speculative — risk score starts at minimum 8

The most critical criteria for Vietnam: #2 (current ratio), #6 (P/E ≤15×), and #9 (debt level). Companies failing all three simultaneously are severely elevated risk.

## 3. Net-Net Analysis

Net-Net analysis is Graham's most conservative valuation method. It is designed for distressed situations where even the business's going-concern value is uncertain, so Planner values only the liquidation value.

### The NCAV Formula

```
NCAV = Current Assets − Total Liabilities (ALL liabilities, not just current)
NCAV per share = NCAV ÷ Total Shares Outstanding
```

Note: Total Liabilities includes BOTH current liabilities AND long-term liabilities. This is more conservative than the current ratio calculation, which only compares current assets to current liabilities.

**Graham's two-thirds rule for net-nets:**
Buy when market price per share < 67% of NCAV per share. The remaining 33% is the safety margin.

### Vietnam Net-Net Candidates

True net-net stocks are rare on HOSE but occasionally appear during:
- Broad market sell-offs (all valuations compressed)
- Sector dislocations (e.g., real estate during 2022-2023 downturn)
- SOE partial privatizations where the state sells at a discount to maintain public perception of accessibility
- Distressed industrial companies with large inventory and receivables

**The Vietnam net-net challenge:** Many Vietnam net-nets stay cheap for years. The typical catalyst for net-net value realization is: SOE privatization completion, asset sale, new controlling shareholder acquiring the business, or a genuine turnaround that improves earnings.

**Planner's net-net rule:** Identify the NCAV, verify the assets are real (receivables are actually collectible, inventory is actually salable), and require a specific catalyst for value realization before recommending entry. "It's cheap" is not sufficient — a catalyst is required even for net-nets.

## 4. Planner's Quality Floor

Planner will not consider any opportunity that fails ALL of these minimum criteria. These are absolute floor conditions — below them, the analysis is not "high risk," it's "not investable."

### The 5 Quality Floor Conditions

**1. Positive operating cash flow for at least 3 consecutive years (5 preferred)**

Earnings can be manipulated; operating cash flow is harder to fake over time. Three consecutive years of positive operating cash flow means the core business genuinely generates more cash than it consumes.

Vietnam exception: SOEs and government-linked companies sometimes show negative FCF during legitimately large capex cycles. In these cases, Planner examines the investment track record — has past capex generated adequate returns?

**2. Debt-to-equity ratio < 0.5**

D/E of 0.5 or lower means equity financing significantly exceeds debt. This provides buffer in a downturn — the company can service debt even if earnings fall significantly.

Vietnam context: Vietnam's banking system has historically financed companies at relatively high D/E ratios. D/E of 1.0-2.0 is common and not automatically fatal, but Planner begins risk scoring from 5+ when D/E exceeds 0.5, and from 7+ when D/E exceeds 1.5.

**3. Current ratio > 1.5 (prefer > 2.0)**

Short-term liquidity buffer. If the current ratio falls below 1.0, the company cannot meet its short-term obligations with current assets — it depends on debt refinancing or asset sales to survive. This is a crisis-level signal.

**4. No controlling shareholder pledge exceeding 20% of their total holdings**

Share pledging by controlling shareholders creates margin call risk. If the stock price falls significantly, the shareholder may be forced to sell pledged shares to meet margin calls, creating a self-reinforcing cascade.

Vietnam specifics: Share pledge information is disclosed in annual reports but may lag by 6-12 months. Brokers sometimes have more current data. When pledge levels cannot be confirmed, flag as unknown risk.

**5. Net debt ≤ 3× EBITDA**

Net debt = total interest-bearing debt minus cash and cash equivalents. EBITDA = earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. A net debt / EBITDA ratio above 3× means the company would take 3+ years of all its operating earnings (before capex, taxes, or debt service) to repay its net debt. This is elevated leverage for Vietnam's opaque environment.

## 5. Intrinsic Value Estimation — Planner's Conservative Method

Planner estimates intrinsic value using the MOST CONSERVATIVE of three methods. This is the opposite of how optimistic analysts work (choosing the method that produces the highest valuation). By using the minimum across three methods, Planner ensures the margin of safety is genuine, not manufactured.

### Method 1: Graham's Normalized P/E

```
Base Intrinsic Value = 3-Year Average EPS × 12
```

The 12× multiple is Graham's conservative benchmark for an average quality company with average prospects. For a company in decline, Planner uses 8-10×. For a company with above-average but not exceptional quality, Planner uses 12-15×.

The 3-year average EPS smooths cyclical distortions — using peak earnings to value a cyclical company leads to overpaying at the top of the cycle.

**Growth adjustment:** Add the present value of the next 5 years' earnings growth, discounted at 10%. Use HALF the analyst consensus growth rate to be conservative. If analysts expect 20% EPS growth, Planner uses 10% for the growth adjustment.

### Method 2: EV/EBITDA Peer Comparison

Calculate the Enterprise Value to EBITDA multiple at which comparable peers trade, then apply a 20% discount to the peer average (Vietnam opacity premium).

```
Conservative EV = Peer EV/EBITDA × 0.80 × Company EBITDA
Conservative Equity Value = Conservative EV − Net Debt
Intrinsic Value Per Share = Conservative Equity Value ÷ Shares Outstanding
```

### Method 3: NCAV (Liquidation Floor)

NCAV = Current Assets − Total Liabilities (all)
NCAV per share = the absolute floor — what the company is worth if liquidated today.

**Planner's rule:** The intrinsic value is the MINIMUM of the three methods. If Method 1 gives 50,000 VND, Method 2 gives 42,000 VND, and Method 3 gives 35,000 VND → Planner's intrinsic value = 35,000 VND. The margin of safety calculation uses this conservative figure.

### Applying the Margin of Safety

Once intrinsic value is established:

```
Maximum Entry Price (33% discount) = Intrinsic Value × 0.67
Maximum Entry Price (50% discount, deep value) = Intrinsic Value × 0.50
```

Vietnam adjustment: Apply 10% additional discount for VAS opacity:
```
Vietnam Adjusted Max Entry = Standard Max Entry × 0.90
```

If the current market price is above the Vietnam-adjusted max entry price → the stock does not have an adequate margin of safety. Planner notes this and the risk score rises.

- Warren Buffett's annual letters (berkshirehathaway.com/letters) — practical application of Graham's framework with modern insights on quality
