# Growth Investing Framework — Seer Reference

Seer's primary mandate is to find opportunity. This document provides the intellectual scaffolding for identifying, analyzing, and validating growth investment theses in the Vietnam market.

---

## 1. GARP — Growth at a Reasonable Price

GARP is Seer's primary valuation lens. It rejects two extremes: pure value investing (which might buy a cheap company that stays cheap forever) and pure growth investing (which might overpay for a great company). GARP seeks the intersection: companies growing fast enough to justify a premium, but not priced for perfection.

### The PEG Ratio

```
PEG = P/E ÷ EPS Growth Rate (as a percentage)
```

The PEG ratio normalizes a P/E ratio for the growth rate. A P/E of 20× sounds expensive — but if EPS is growing at 25% per year, the PEG is 0.80, which is actually cheap by GARP standards.

**Interpretation:**
- PEG < 1.0: The stock is undervalued relative to its growth rate — classic GARP entry
- PEG 1.0–1.5: Fair value for a quality growth company — acceptable entry if the business is high quality
- PEG > 1.5: Paying a premium for growth — requires exceptional quality or an extraordinary catalyst
- PEG > 2.0: Priced for perfection — risk of significant multiple compression if growth disappoints

**Use forward P/E, not trailing.** Trailing P/E lags growth inflections. A company growing EPS 50% will look expensive on trailing P/E but reasonable on next-year forward P/E. Seer always uses consensus forward estimates when available, and notes when only trailing data is accessible.

### Vietnam P/E Context

The VN-Index historically trades at 12–15× trailing P/E. This is lower than regional peers (Thailand at 15–18×, Indonesia at 14–17×) due to Vietnam's lower liquidity, VAS opacity, and foreign ownership constraints.

**Vietnam GARP benchmarks:**
- VN-Index average P/E (trailing): 12–15×
- Quality large-cap (VN30 components): 15–20× justified
- High-growth mid-cap (20%+ EPS CAGR): 20–25× forward P/E is still GARP if growth sustains
- Any stock above 25× forward P/E: requires exceptional catalyst clarity — flag in the thesis

### Vietnam Growth Premium Sectors (Where GARP is Most Relevant)

The following sectors have historically supported GARP valuations in Vietnam because structural growth drivers are visible and durable:

- **Manufacturing FDI beneficiaries:** Industrial parks, supporting industries, logistics. Driven by China+1 sourcing shifts.
- **Fintech and banking:** Underpenetrated financial services. Credit card, insurance, and digital banking penetration remain low — long runway.
- **Healthcare:** Aging demographics, rising middle class, increasing private healthcare spend.
- **Consumer discretionary:** Modern retail replacing traditional channels (wet markets → supermarkets → e-commerce).
- **Logistics:** E-commerce growth driving warehousing and last-mile demand.
- **Green energy:** Government renewable energy targets creating regulatory demand for solar and wind development.

## 2. Peter Lynch's Stock Categories

Peter Lynch's framework from "One Up on Wall Street" (1989) provides Seer with a simple but powerful typology. Different categories have different GARP parameters and different risk/reward profiles.

### Slow Growers

**Characteristics:** Revenue and EPS growing at 4–8% per year, roughly in line with GDP. Often mature, dividend-paying companies. Utility-like.

**Valuation:** These trade at 8–12× P/E. The upside from multiple expansion is limited.

**Seer's posture:** NOT Seer's hunting ground. Slow growers are Planner's territory — they are capital preservation vehicles, not growth opportunities. If a company is a slow grower, Seer should note this and lower the conviction score accordingly (maximum conviction for a slow grower: 5/10, unless there is a specific catalyst like turnaround or asset monetization).

---

### Stalwarts

**Characteristics:** 10–20% EPS CAGR. Blue-chip quality. Well-known brands or dominant market positions. Examples in Vietnam: VNM (dairy), REE (infrastructure services), FPT (technology).

**Valuation:** These trade at 15–22× P/E. Multiple expansion is possible but modest.

**Seer's entry strategy:** Buy at corrections (when the market punishes the stock for a mediocre quarter, not a structural problem). Target 30–50% total return over 12–18 months. Exit when valuation returns to the high end of the fair range.

**Lynch's stalwart rule:** Sell when you've made 30–50%. Don't try to hold for a 5× return — stalwarts don't deliver that. Move on to the next opportunity.

---

### Fast Growers — Seer's Primary Domain

**Characteristics:** 20%+ EPS CAGR, often small-to-mid cap, in an expansion phase — more distribution, more products, more markets. This is where 5–10× returns are possible over a multi-year hold.

**Valuation:** Premium P/E justified (20–30× forward), but the PEG should still be ≤1.5.

**Seer's fast grower criteria:**
- Is the company in a large, underpenetrated market? (If the market is already 80% penetrated, there's no fast growth to capture)
- Is the growth organic? (Acquisition-driven growth is less durable and harder to analyze)
- Are profit margins expanding as the company scales? (Operating leverage is the sign of a genuine fast grower)
- Is management reinvesting in growth rather than paying dividends? (Fast growers should be building, not distributing)

**Lynch's key risk for fast growers:** Overexpansion. Fast growers often stumble when they expand into unfamiliar segments or geographic markets. Watch for diversification that doesn't make strategic sense.

**Vietnam fast grower examples (sectors, not specific picks):** Industrial park developers during FDI waves, mobile VAS companies during smartphone penetration, retail chains during modern retail expansion phase.

---

### Cyclicals

**Characteristics:** Revenue and earnings move with the economic cycle. Steel, cement, fertilizers, real estate. At the peak of the cycle, P/E looks low (high earnings); at the trough, P/E looks high (depressed earnings).

**Lynch's contrarian rule for cyclicals:** Buy at high P/E (when earnings are depressed and the cycle is about to turn up). Sell at low P/E (when earnings are at the peak and the cycle is about to turn down). This is counter-intuitive — most investors do the opposite and lose money.

**Seer's cyclical approach:** Identify where we are in the cycle. If earnings are near multi-year lows and the cycle indicators (commodity prices, credit growth, construction starts) are turning, this is a cyclical buy. Use normalized EPS (average over a full cycle) as the true earnings power.

**Vietnam cyclical candidates:** Steel (HPG, NKG), cement (BCC, HT1), fertilizers (DPM, DCM), construction materials, residential real estate developers.

---

### Turnarounds

**Characteristics:** Distressed or declining company with a credible plan to recover. Binary risk/reward — big winner or total loss.

**Seer's turnaround criteria (all 3 must be met before building a thesis):**
1. Clear catalyst for the turnaround (new management, asset sale, debt restructuring, regulatory resolution)
2. Balance sheet that can survive the recovery period (minimum 6 months of operating cash runway)
3. Identifiable path to profitability (not just "maybe costs will go down")

**Vietnam turnaround opportunities:** SOEs under equitization with asset monetization potential, real estate developers resolving legal/land issues, companies emerging from debt restructuring.

---

### Asset Plays

**Characteristics:** Companies whose underlying assets are worth more than the market cap — often because the assets aren't visible on the income statement or the market doesn't know how to value them.

**Vietnam asset play categories:**
- **SOE privatizations:** State selling a stake at a discount to book value; the market gradually discovers the true asset value
- **Land-bank companies:** Companies holding large undeveloped land banks in areas of future development (industrial zones, satellite cities)
- **Cash-rich companies:** Companies with large net cash positions that the market is ignoring

**Seer's asset play check:** Calculate sum-of-parts (SOP) valuation. If SOP is more than 30% above market cap, this is a credible asset play thesis.

## 3. Philip Fisher's Qualitative Growth Criteria

Philip Fisher's "Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits" (1958), Chapter 3, provides 15 qualitative criteria for evaluating growth companies. These are harder to score quantitatively than PEG ratios — they require judgment. Seer applies the most relevant 8 criteria for Vietnam.

### The 8 Fisher Criteria Most Relevant for Vietnam

**1. Does the company have products or services with sufficient market potential to make possible a sizable increase in sales for at least several years?**

The size of the total addressable market (TAM) matters. A company that is already 40% of its market cannot grow at 20% for long. In Vietnam's fast-developing economy, look for companies in markets that are 10-20% penetrated — healthcare, modern logistics, financial services, EVs.

**2. Does the company have a determination to continue developing new products or services to further increase sales potential?**

A one-product company growing fast will eventually hit a wall. The best fast growers have a pipeline — next-generation products, new service lines, geographic expansion. In Vietnam, this often means a company that dominates HCMC moving to Hanoi, or a company in the B2B space adding B2C channels.

**3. How effective is the company's research and development effort?**

In Vietnam's manufacturing and technology context, this translates to: is the company moving up the value chain, or does it remain a low-cost assembler? Companies investing in design and automation rather than just low-cost labor have durable competitive advantage.

**4. Does the company have an above-average sales organization?**

In Vietnam, distribution is often the moat — not the product. A company with 10,000 distribution points is much harder to disrupt than one with 1,000. Look at market coverage, not just market share.

**5. Does the company have a worthwhile profit margin?**

Is operating margin above 10%? Is it expanding or contracting? Margin expansion as revenue scales = operating leverage = the sign of a genuinely profitable business model. Margin compression despite revenue growth = structural problem.

**6. What is the company doing to maintain or improve its profit margins?**

Specifically: is the company controlling costs, investing in automation, or just raising prices? In Vietnam's inflationary environment (logistics costs, labor costs rising), companies with pricing power or cost efficiency initiatives are more durable.

**7. Does the company have outstanding labor relations?**

In Vietnam's manufacturing context: what is the turnover rate? Are there labor disputes? Companies with stable workforces (particularly in electronics assembly, garment manufacturing) have lower training costs and higher productivity.

**8. Does the company have depth of management?**

The "key man risk" is real in Vietnam's family-controlled business environment. If the company's performance depends entirely on one founder/patriarch, a health crisis or loss of government favor can be devastating. Look for a management team that has independent capability across CFO, COO, and business unit leads.

### Vietnam-Specific Fisher Weights

Due to VAS opacity and the typical Vietnam listed company structure (family-controlled, low disclosure), Seer weights these Fisher criteria most heavily:

- **Management communication with shareholders** (transparency in earnings calls, disclosures, related-party transaction clarity): HIGH WEIGHT. Opaque management is a red flag.
- **Capital allocation track record**: Does management return cash to shareholders when they run out of high-return reinvestment opportunities? Or do they make empire-building acquisitions? HIGH WEIGHT.
- **Depth of management bench**: Is there a capable #2? LOW WEIGHT if the founder is genuinely exceptional and relatively young.

## 4. Vietnam Growth Sectors — Where Seer Looks First

These sectors have structural growth drivers visible enough for multi-year thesis construction.

### Manufacturing FDI Beneficiaries
**Investment thesis:** Vietnam is the primary beneficiary of China+1 supply chain diversification. Electronics (Samsung, LG, Intel), textile, furniture, and supporting industries are relocating capacity.

**Who benefits:** Industrial park developers (KBC, SZL, BCM, VGC), logistics and warehousing (GMD, STG, VTP), and component manufacturers that serve the FDI tenants.

**Thesis duration:** 5-10 years; secular trend, not a single-year catalyst.

**Watch for:** Land bank availability, infrastructure quality in northern industrial provinces (Bac Ninh, Bac Giang, Hai Phong).

---

### Financial Services
**Investment thesis:** Vietnam's financial penetration is among the lowest in Southeast Asia. Credit card ownership, insurance penetration, and digital banking adoption have long runways.

**Who benefits:** Private banks during credit expansion phases (TCB, VPB, MBB), insurance companies (BVH, ABI, PTI), payment infrastructure.

**Key metrics:** Net interest margin (NIM) trends, CASA ratio (higher = cheaper funding = better margins), NPL trajectory, credit growth relative to SBV credit quota.

---

### Consumer Discretionary — Modern Retail
**Investment thesis:** Vietnam's middle class is expanding; shopping is shifting from traditional channels to modern retail and e-commerce.

**Who benefits:** Mobile retail (MWG, FRT), F&B consumer brands (MSN Consumer division), supermarket chains (Saigon Co.op — not listed, but competitors are).

**Key metrics:** Same-store sales growth (SSSG) — the most important measure of organic performance for retail chains.

---

### Healthcare
**Investment thesis:** Aging population + rising income = growing demand for private healthcare, pharmaceuticals, diagnostics.

**Who benefits:** Pharmaceutical manufacturers and distributors (DVN, IMP, DHG), private hospital groups, medical device distributors.

**Key metrics:** Revenue growth vs. pricing power, generic vs. branded mix (branded = higher margin, but more regulatory risk).

---

### Green Energy
**Investment thesis:** Vietnam government has committed to aggressive renewable energy targets. Solar and wind development is explicitly mandated.

**Who benefits:** Companies with approved capacity, land permits, and power purchase agreements (PPAs) with EVN (state electricity company).

**Key risk:** Government feed-in tariff policy has been inconsistent. Regulatory reversals are a genuine risk — Seer should always flag this in any green energy thesis.
