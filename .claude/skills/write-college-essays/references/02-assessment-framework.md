# 02 — Assessment Framework: How to Evaluate Any Essay

## The 5-Dimension Rubric

Score each dimension 1–5. Total score determines action.

| Score | Action |
|---|---|
| **20–25** | Strong — proceed to polish; focus on sentence rhythm and word choice |
| **15–19** | Needs revision — identify the lowest-scoring dimension and restructure around it |
| **Below 15** | Rethink the approach — the anchor story, structure, or arc may need to change |

---

### Dimension 1: Authenticity (1–5)

**Question:** Could only you write this?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | A specific experience, moment, or insight no other applicant could claim; the writer's fingerprints are on every sentence |
| 4 | Mostly specific; one or two passages could apply to others but the overall essay is distinctive |
| 3 | Genuine but generic in places; the setup could belong to many people even if the detail is individual |
| 2 | Relies on common experiences (team projects, academic challenges, "overcoming adversity") without a distinctive lens |
| 1 | Could have been submitted by any of a thousand applicants; no moment or voice distinguishes this writer |

**Fail signal:** "I have always been passionate about..." / "This experience was transformative..." / any opening that a stranger could have written.
**Pass signal:** A specific moment no one else could claim.

---

### Dimension 2: Specificity (1–5)

**Question:** Are claims backed by evidence?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Every assertion is grounded in concrete detail; no unearned claims; the reader can picture every scene |
| 4 | Most claims are evidenced; one or two assertions are left as summaries rather than scenes |
| 3 | Mix of specific and vague; reader occasionally asked to take the writer's word for things |
| 2 | Mostly assertions; few scenes; the reader knows what the writer concludes but not what they experienced |
| 1 | Pure assertion throughout; no evidence of the specific experiences claimed |

**Fail signal:** "I learned leadership." / "The experience taught me resilience." (assertion without evidence)
**Pass signal:** The reader can reconstruct the scene — time, place, stakes, what happened — from the words on the page.

---

### Dimension 3: Arc (1–5)

**Question:** Do we know the writer better at the end than at the beginning?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Clear before/during/after movement; the reader can name the specific shift in thinking, belief, or behavior |
| 4 | Arc is present but the "after" is slightly underdeveloped; the growth is visible but not fully landed |
| 3 | Some sense of change but the reader cannot clearly name what changed; the essay ends where it started in feeling |
| 2 | No visible arc; the essay is a chronological list of accomplishments with no turning point |
| 1 | The writer at the end is identical to the writer at the beginning; the essay is a report |

**Fail signal:** A list of accomplishments; no moment where something changes.
**Pass signal:** The reader could complete this sentence after finishing: "I was X. Then Y happened. Now I understand Z."

---

### Dimension 4: Voice (1–5)

**Question:** Does this sound like a human being?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Natural rhythm throughout; strong verbs; varied sentence length; the writer's personality is audible |
| 4 | Mostly strong; occasional jargon or passive voice construction that mutes the personality |
| 3 | Serviceable but corporate or academic in register; would not identify this writer by voice alone |
| 2 | Heavy with jargon, passive voice, or adverb-weak verb pairs; reads like a performance review |
| 1 | Could have been written by anyone; flat, interchangeable, robotic |

**Fail signal:** Passive voice dominant; adverbs paired with weak verbs; business jargon ("leverage," "synergy," "bandwidth"); sentence lengths identical throughout.
**Pass signal:** Natural rhythm; strong active verbs; sentences vary from one word to thirty; the writer's personality is detectable in the prose.

---

### Dimension 5: Relevance (1–5)

**Question:** Does every paragraph serve the prompt's core ask?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Every sentence advances the answer to the actual prompt; no detours into adjacent topics |
| 4 | Mostly on-prompt; one paragraph or passage feels like a tangent the writer couldn't cut |
| 3 | Answers the prompt but also wanders into content that wasn't requested; the reader has to do some work |
| 2 | Partially answers the prompt; other material dominates; the writer seems to be answering a different question |
| 1 | Doesn't answer the prompt; the writer answered the question they wished they had been asked |

**Fail signal:** Answering the question you wished they had asked.
**Pass signal:** Every paragraph could justify its presence by pointing directly to a specific word in the prompt.

---

## The Thumb Test (from 50 Ivy League Essays)

Cover the applicant's name. Ask: **could 1,000 other applicants submit this essay?**

If yes: rewrite. The thumb test fails when:
- Any sentence begins "I have always believed..."
- Any sentence begins "This experience taught me..."
- The central experience is a generic category (team project, academic struggle, travel experience) without a specific, irreplaceable lens
- The insight at the close is a platitude ("hard work pays off," "teamwork matters," "I learned to be open-minded")

The thumb test passes when: removing the applicant's name makes the essay meaningless, because the details are so specific they could only belong to one person.

---

## The "Why" Depth Test

Ask "why" five times on any stated motivation or claim. If you cannot answer by the fifth iteration, the essay has not gone deep enough. The real story is usually at layer four or five.

**Example chain:**
1. "I want the IMBA." → *Why?*
2. "To move into business strategy." → *Why?*
3. "Because I hit a ceiling at the product level." → *Why did you hit a ceiling?*
4. "Because Ripid showed me I couldn't answer the business-level questions." → *Why did that matter?*
5. "Because I realized I was optimizing the wrong layer — the product worked; the business underneath it didn't." → **Now you have an essay.**

The depth test is also a structure tool. Layer 5 is usually the opening thesis of the best version of the essay.

## Red Flags Checklist

**Banned opening moves:**
- Question: "Have you ever wondered what it means to lead?"
- Famous quote: "As Albert Einstein once said..."
- Dictionary definition: "Leadership is defined as..."
- "Since I was a child, I always knew I wanted to..."
- "In this essay, I will discuss..."

**Banned words and phrases:**
- passion / unique / hardworking / dedicated / transformative
- leverage (as a verb) / synergy / thinking outside the box / bandwidth / win-win
- "alright" (in formal writing)
- utilize (use *use*) / necessitate (use *require*) / conceptualize (use *imagine*)

**Banned moves:**
- Listing credentials without context or story
- Resume regurgitation (restating the CV in paragraph form)
- Over-sentimentality without insight ("I was so moved by this experience")
- Vague insight: "this experience changed my perspective" — on what? how?

**Grammar traps:**
- Passive voice throughout (look for "was" + past participle constructions)
- its/it's confusion
- Vague antecedents: "this," "it," "which" without a clear referent
- Adverb + weak verb pairs: "worked diligently" → *executed*; "communicated effectively" → *aligned*

---

## Green Signals Checklist

An essay is on track when:
- Opens in a specific moment with at least one sensory or concrete detail
- Has an organizing anchor: one object, one metaphor, one through-line
- Contains at least one moment of genuine internal conflict or honest admission of ignorance
- Closes with a specific insight, not a summary or a wish
- Sentence lengths vary: the longest sentence is at least 3× the length of the shortest
- Every paragraph would survive a "does this earn its place?" test — nothing is kept for padding
