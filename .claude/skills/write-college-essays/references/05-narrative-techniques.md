# 05 — Narrative Techniques: Structuring the Full Arc

## The Gelb Framework (4 Elements)

Every strong essay contains all four in sequence. If any is missing, the essay is incomplete.

1. **"The Once"** — A specific, compressed moment (minutes, not years)
2. **The Extraordinary** — Something breaks the ordinary pattern; demands a response
3. **Tension and Conflict** — What is at stake; who or what is in conflict?
4. **The Point** — The insight gained; the changed understanding; the new self

The four elements do not have to appear in exactly this order, but they must all be present. The "Point" can be distributed throughout rather than saved entirely for the end — as long as the full insight lands before the final sentence.

## The Before / During / After Arc

The single most reliable structure for any admission essay of 300 words or more.

| Phase | Question | What to Write |
|---|---|---|
| **Before** | Who were you? What did you believe? What did you assume? | Establish the writer's starting state — not as a summary, but as a specific belief or assumption |
| **During** | What happened? What disrupted? What was the moment of reckoning? | The compressed scene; the conflict; the moment the previous assumption broke |
| **After** | What changed? What do you understand now? What is the evidence of that change? | The specific new behavior, question, or belief — shown, not announced |

**Shorthand check:** After completing a draft, complete this sentence: *"I was X. Then Y happened. Now I understand Z."*

If you cannot complete the sentence, the arc is incomplete. If X and Z are identical, there is no arc — only a report.

## Showing Growth Without Over-Claiming

The most common essay failure: announcing the growth rather than demonstrating it.

**Never say:** "This experience transformed me completely." / "I grew so much from this." / "I became a better leader." These are conclusions the reader should reach independently, from the evidence. When the writer announces them, the reader's critical faculty activates — they begin looking for proof, and if the proof was never shown, the claim collapses.

**Always show:** A specific changed behavior. A different question you now ask. A decision you made differently after the experience than you would have made before it.

**Example construction:**
> "I stopped waiting for sign-off on decisions I had the authority to make. The first time I issued the go-ahead without escalating, my hands were shaking. The second time, they were not."

This is a changed behavior, not a claimed insight. The reader concludes "this person grew more confident in their authority" from the evidence — the writer does not need to say it. The hands detail is what makes it specific. Without the hands, it is a declaration. With the hands, it is a scene.

## The Single Through-Line

Essays over 400 words need one organizing idea threading through every section. Without a through-line, the essay reads as a collection of incidents — competent, perhaps, but not cohesive.

**How to identify your through-line:** Name it in five words. If you cannot, the essay lacks one.

**Examples of viable through-lines:**
- A skill: discipline, precision, translation, navigation
- A metaphor: bridges, integration, maps, infrastructure
- A recurring question: "what does the business actually require?" / "where do institutions decide to move?"
- A tension: product vs. business; execution vs. strategy; doing vs. directing

**The Nisha D. test:** Dance discipline appears in childhood, in college, in career, and in community service — one thread, never broken, never forced. Each section of the essay uses it differently, which prevents repetition while maintaining coherence. If the through-line appears in every paragraph but means the same thing each time, it is repetition. If it means something slightly different each time — applied to a new domain, gaining new depth — it is cohesion.

**Test for coherence:** Point to the through-line in each paragraph. If a paragraph does not touch it, either the paragraph doesn't belong or the through-line is not the right one.

## Proportion and Pacing

Poor proportion is the most common structural problem in essays written by strong writers. Good writers often produce too much backstory — they feel the context is necessary, and some of it is. But most readers need less context than writers think they do.

**Suggested proportions for a 400–500 word essay:**

| Section | Word Count | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Opening scene / hook | 80–100 words | ~20% |
| Development / tension / the story | 150–200 words | ~35–40% |
| Reflection / insight / the learning | 100–120 words | ~25% |
| Closing and forward-looking | 60–80 words | ~15% |

**For 300-word essays:** Cut backstory entirely. Open in the middle of the insight. The reader does not need context before the scene; the scene provides its own context.

**The 70/30 warning:** If more than 70% of the essay is backstory and scene-setting, the "Point" — the insight and growth — is being crowded out. Admissions readers are not primarily reading for what happened to you. They are reading for how you think about what happened to you.

## The Graduate School Distinction (Asher)

Graduate essays are governed by a different contract than undergraduate essays.

**Undergraduate essays** ask: Who are you as a person? What shaped you? The personal narrative is the end in itself.

**Graduate essays** ask: How has your experience shaped your thinking and your goals? The personal narrative serves as *evidence* for professional judgment — not as the destination.

This distinction has practical consequences:
- The emotional register is slightly lower in graduate essays; professional insight is the currency
- The "After" phase of the arc must explicitly connect to a professional or intellectual capability, not just a personal one
- The reader is asking: does this person think clearly about their own experience? Can they extract transferable lessons from specific events?

**Asher's formulation:** As you move up the academic ladder, essays become "more precise, more specific, and less personal." Not impersonal — but the personal detail now serves the professional claim.

**Applied:** In an MBA essay, the Ripid story is not about the emotional experience of building and winding down a startup. It is about the specific gap in strategic capability that the experience revealed, and the evidence that the writer has diagnosed that gap accurately. The emotion is present; it is not the point.
