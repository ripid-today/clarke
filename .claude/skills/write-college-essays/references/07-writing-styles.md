# 07 — Writing Styles: Style A and Style B

## When to Choose

| Situation | Recommended Style |
|---|---|
| Prompt invites personal story, reflection, or community experience | Style A |
| Prompt demands professional trajectory, goals, or analytical clarity | Style B |
| Long essay with room to build scene and land strategy | Hybrid (open A, close B) |
| Short essay (300 words or less) | Style B — no room for extended scene-building |

**How to invoke:** The user says "use Style A," "use Style B," or "open Style A, close Style B." If no style is specified, ask before drafting.

---

## STYLE A: Narrative / Immersive

### Philosophy

The story is the argument. The reader arrives at the conclusion because they have lived the scene, not because the writer announced it. The insight is delivered through experience, not stated as a thesis.

### Characteristics

- Opens in medias res with a specific, sensory detail (time, place, object, sound)
- Uses one organizing metaphor or symbol that can return at the close
- Long sentences for immersion, reflection, and scene-building; short sentences for impact and arrival
- Deliberate tempo shifts: first gear (reflective), second gear (momentum), third gear (impact)
- Conflict is internal — doubt, the gap between knowing and not knowing, the reckoning with a previous assumption
- Dialogue used sparingly, only at the single most pivotal moment
- Closes by returning to the opening detail, now transformed in meaning — the bookend structure

### Style A Self-Check

Before submitting a Style A draft:
- Is there at least one image the reader can picture without any imagination required?
- Is the central tension internal — a gap, a contradiction, a moment of honest reckoning?
- Does the closing land the insight without announcing it?
- Is there a through-line that appears at the opening and returns, transformed, at the close?
- Do the sentence lengths vary by at least a factor of three?

### Constructed Example (Ripid context)

> "The morning I decided to move Ripid in a new direction, the app still had two thousand people opening it — people who had, somewhere in the aggregate of those small daily check-ins, finished their first book, built their first consistent morning, found a stranger who ran the same distance at the same hour. I had built something that worked. What I had not built was a business I could answer for. The financial model existed as a feeling, not a document. The growth decision existed as intuition, not a framework. I could see the product. I could not see the institution the product required to survive."

**What to notice:**
- Sensory grounding: "two thousand people opening it" — specific, visual, present-tense in its weight
- Specific human impact: "finished their first book," "found a stranger who ran the same distance" — the reader can picture individual people
- Parallel sentence structures expose the gap with precision: *feeling vs. document*, *intuition vs. framework*, *see the product vs. see the institution*
- No announcement of insight — the insight is built into the parallelism; the reader arrives there naturally
- The through-line (the gap between product and business) is introduced here and will need to thread through the full essay

## STYLE B: Strategic / Forward-Looking

### Philosophy

The argument is explicit. The evidence comes from experience. The logic moves forward. The reader should be able to follow the reasoning without effort — the essay does not make them decode it.

### Characteristics

- Opens with the professional claim or the strategic question, not a scene
- Structure: *experience → what it revealed → what it demands → what the program provides*
- Sentences are medium length, active, precise — no embedded clauses that bury the main verb
- Jargon-free but professional in register: sounds like a senior practitioner, not a business school applicant
- Connects every paragraph to a specific career claim or gap
- Closes with a specific, forward-looking statement — not a wish, but a plan; not "I hope to," but "I will" or "the program enables"

### Style B Self-Check

Before submitting a Style B draft:
- Is the central claim stated in the first or second sentence?
- Is every cited experience evidence for a specific capability, gap, or decision — not general context?
- Does the closing name a specific outcome, not a general aspiration?
- Does the essay avoid listing program features as if reading a brochure?
- Would a senior banker reading this believe the writer has earned the right to make these claims?

### Constructed Example (career pivot context)

> "Three years of building bank integrations at MoMo taught me how payments move — through regulations, across system boundaries, between institutions that trust each other by treaty if not by preference. What they did not teach me is how a banking organization decides where to move. I can execute at the product layer. I cannot yet operate at the strategy layer. The IMBA at NTU is the bridge: twelve months inside Asia's most interconnected financial market, building the vocabulary and the rigor the second layer requires."

**What to notice:**
- Clear professional claim upfront: three years, specific work, specific company — the writer earns the right to speak before making the claim
- The central gap is named precisely: *execute at the product layer* vs. *operate at the strategy layer* — two concrete things, not a vague aspiration
- Specific evidence: "regulations," "system boundaries," "institutions that trust each other by treaty if not by preference" — this is not generic fintech language; it is specific to bank integration work
- The program is positioned as *the bridge*, tied directly to the gap — not as a general credential
- No generic phrases: no "broaden my horizons," no "network with industry leaders," no "world-class faculty"
- The closing names a specific outcome (vocabulary and rigor for the strategy layer) connected to a specific asset (twelve months in Asia's most interconnected financial market)

## Hybrid Mode: Open A, Close B

The hybrid is often the strongest approach for essays of 400–500 words where both personal depth and strategic clarity are required.

**Structure:**
1. Open with Style A — a specific scene or moment that establishes the gap or the insight (first 100–150 words)
2. Pivot sentence — moves from interior reckoning to exterior claim (one to two sentences)
3. Continue with Style B — evidence, diagnosis, program connection (middle 200–250 words)
4. Close with Style B — the specific forward-looking statement (last 60–80 words)

**The pivot sentence is the hinge.** It is often the single hardest sentence to write in a hybrid essay. It must carry the reader from the emotional register of Style A into the analytical register of Style B without the transition feeling abrupt.

**Constructed pivot sentences:**
- "What that midnight call clarified was not a technical problem. It was a diagnostic one: I had been trained to fix the system; I had not been trained to evaluate whether it was the right system."
- "The experience did not change my goals. It changed my ability to articulate what they would actually require."
- "What Ripid revealed was not a product gap. It was a vocabulary gap — and vocabulary, in strategy, is not decoration."
