# 03 — Sentence Craft: Tempo, Rhythm, and Word Quality

## The Sentence Length Principle

Long sentences and short sentences do different work. Neither is inherently better. The ratio between them determines whether an essay has energy or lies flat.

- **Long sentences:** build momentum, create contemplative pace, carry complex thoughts, describe scenes and render reflection
- **Short sentences:** land with force, shift emotional register, shock the reader awake, conclude a thought with finality

**Formula:** Use long sentences to set scenes and to think. Use short sentences to arrive.

**Constructed example (Ripid/MoMo spirit):**
> "Every integration meant months of calls with compliance officers, legal teams, and bank APIs that returned error codes no documentation had anticipated, and I learned to treat each failure not as a setback but as a map of exactly what the system had not yet been asked to do. Then one night, everything broke. I did not escalate. I fixed it."

Notice: one long sentence builds context and introduces the philosophy of treating failure as a map. One short sentence shifts register. Two punchy single-clause structures deliver the resolution. The reader feels the contrast between complexity and decisiveness.

## The Three Gears — Changing Tempo

Every paragraph has a gear. Identifying the gear and shifting deliberately is the mark of a controlled writer.

**First gear (slow, reflective):**
- Embedded clauses, soft language, internal thought
- Use for: insight moments, moments of reflection, the pause before the turn
- *"What the experience ultimately revealed was a gap I had not anticipated — not in the product, which worked, but in my ability to construct the architecture beneath it."*

**Second gear (narrative momentum):**
- Medium sentences, active verbs, action unfolding in sequence
- Use for: story progression, the during-phase, building toward the turn
- *"I convened the teams. I drafted the checklist. I walked the bank's compliance officer through every field in the API contract until she signed."*

**Third gear (impact):**
- One to five words maximum. A single line. The arrival.
- Use for: the turn, the reveal, the closing beat
- *"Then the numbers dropped."* or *"I had been optimizing the wrong layer."*

**Writing instruction:** Identify the three gears in every paragraph you write. If all sentences are in the same gear, the paragraph is flat. Paragraphs that never shift gears read like corporate reports; paragraphs that shift gears read like prose.

## Strong Verbs Over Adverbs (the Gelb Rule)

**Rule:** If you need an adverb to strengthen a verb, find a stronger verb instead.

The adverb signals the writer hasn't committed to the most precise word. The adverb is a symptom, not a solution.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| walked slowly and carefully | crept |
| said nervously | stammered |
| worked hard to coordinate | convened, aligned, escalated, resolved |
| communicated effectively | aligned (on strategy); clarified (the requirement); translated (between teams) |
| moved quickly to address | intervened / acted |
| thought carefully about | interrogated / reckoned with |

**Quality verb bank (use when the common verb is too weak):**
sift, unravel, pivot, anchor, dismantle, interrogate, forge, wrestle, converge, reckon, surface, reveal, confront, reframe, expose, reconstruct, translate, broker, navigate, absorb

## Word Choice Quality

**Precision over flourish.** The word that means exactly what you intend always beats the impressive-sounding word.

**One-syllable strength:** Short words often land harder than long ones.
- "I cut the timeline" lands harder than "I implemented a reduction in the project duration"
- "I built it" lands harder than "I developed and deployed the implementation"
- "The bank called" lands harder than "We received notification from the financial institution"

**Nominalization test (convert nominalizations to verbs):**
- "make a decision" → *decide*
- "have an understanding of" → *understand*
- "provide assistance to" → *help*
- "give consideration to" → *consider*
- "reach a conclusion" → *conclude*
- "conduct an investigation" → *investigate*

**Never use these words in any admission essay:**
- *utilize* (use *use*)
- *necessitate* (use *require*)
- *conceptualize* (use *imagine* or *envision*)
- *leverage* as a verb
- *dialogue* as a verb
- *impactful*
- *robust* (to describe a system, plan, or process)

**The read-aloud test:** If you stumble reading a sentence aloud, the sentence needs rewriting. Your mouth finds the awkward constructions before your eyes do. Read every draft aloud at least once.

## Dialogue — Use Sparingly, Strategically (Gelb)

Gelb's exact framing: dialogue is "spice, not stew." It is never used for exposition — to deliver context or information. It is used only at pivotal moments, to reveal character in a single exchange.

**Rules:**
- Always use "said" — never "declared triumphantly," "whispered conspiratorially," or "exclaimed enthusiastically"
- Keep it short: 3 lines maximum. Longer dialogue is driving the plot, not illuminating character.
- The dialogue must reveal who the speaker is in a single exchange, not advance the narrative or deliver information

**When to use it:**
- The moment a decision became real
- The moment someone said exactly the thing that clarified the conflict
- The turning point in a negotiation or conversation

**When not to use it:**
- To explain context (use narration instead)
- To show that people talked (summarize instead)
- To fill space with quoted material

## Metaphor and Symbol

One strong organizing metaphor beats many scattered metaphors. The metaphor must illuminate, not decorate.

**Test:** Remove the metaphor. If nothing changes in meaning, the metaphor was decoration. If the sentence loses precision, the metaphor was doing real work.

**Book examples that work:**
- *Dance discipline (Nisha D.):* Discipline required in Bharatanatyam maps onto discipline in academics, career, and community service. The metaphor is not forced because both domains genuinely require the same thing.
- *Red glasses (Sidd B.):* The glasses are a physical object that tracks the writer's evolution across time. The reader can picture them; the symbolism is built gradually, not announced.
- *Ripple effects (Grace M.):* The foster family experience mirrors the investment thesis. Two domains share a mechanism; the metaphor holds because the mechanism is the same in both.

**Mixed metaphors are a cardinal error.** They reveal a writer not reading their own prose.
- Wrong: "I climbed the ladder until I hit a slippery slope"
- Wrong: "We built a bridge to the finish line"
- Catch mixed metaphors by reading the essay aloud; they are easier to hear than to see.

**Symbol tip (Gelb):** An object the writer can return to in the closing creates a satisfying bookend. Gelb's example is a dime from the broken shopping bag scene. When the dime reappears at the end, the essay achieves closure that a stated conclusion cannot.
