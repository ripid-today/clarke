## Q2 — Performance vs. Peers + Principal Strengths

In four years at MoMo, Nguyen Hong Nguyen stands among the strongest data-driven product members I have managed. MoMo operates at scale across major payment verticals, and the bar for product rigor on the Bank Integrations team is deliberately high — I do not say this routinely. What separates him from other capable members is not analytical skill alone, but the combination of analytical courage, structured self-development, and a refusal to set aside findings when they happen to be inconvenient.

The clearest evidence of his analytical courage came during our online payment behavior optimization project, when the team had already coalesced around a complex, multi-phase solution after months of internal deliberation. Nguyen ran the data independently and arrived at a different conclusion: a simpler intervention could achieve comparable impact without the added cost and timeline risk. He then did something most people in his position would not — he brought that finding forward, despite the existing proposal having earned buy-in from product, engineering, operations, and business development, and despite knowing that multiple departments had already invested planning time in the original path. Rather than softening his recommendation, he built a data-grounded narrative, scheduled individual time with each stakeholder, and held his position through every round of pushback until the team had enough evidence to shift course. The feature shipped on schedule and delivered the projected outcome.

What this confirmed, and what I have since observed across other projects, is that Nguyen does not treat data as a tool for validating directions already chosen. He treats it as a compass, following where it points even when the destination is uncomfortable for everyone in the room.

His second quality, structured perseverance in learning, became visible early in his tenure. Nguyen joined MoMo without a fintech background, yet rather than waiting for gaps to close informally, he mapped every data field used across engineering, data, and product — documenting definitions, relationships, and how each field appeared across different product contexts — and turned that effort into a shared knowledge base his teammates use today. That friction had existed before he arrived. Most teams eventually learn to work around it. He eliminated it.

What both qualities share is that they reduce the distance between analysis and decision: Nguyen's recommendations are unusually likely to survive contact with implementation because he has already tested them against every stakeholder before the decision is formally made. For NTU IMBA cohorts, the most durable contributors are those who bring data discipline without rigidity, build assets that outlast the projects that created them, and persist past the point where most people defer to existing consensus. Nguyen is precisely that profile.

## Q3 — Most Important Constructive Feedback

Of all my feedback conversations with Nguyen over two years, one stays with me — not for the project it concerned, but for what his response revealed. The occasion was our online payment behavior optimization project, where Nguyen was leading the data analysis workstream with the kind of depth that ultimately made his recommendation credible, but where I noticed, as the project progressed, that his genuine interest in the problem had begun to work against its momentum: as the complexity deepened, the analysis expanded with it, and the transition from investigation to delivery was stretching further than the timeline could absorb. He was thorough in a way that had real value, and thorough in a way that was becoming a cost.

I met with him and said: the analysis had crossed from productive into self-sustaining. When you care about a problem, it is easy to keep following the next question without anchoring the depth of your inquiry to a defined outcome, and depth without a stopping point is not rigor — it is a liability to the team depending on the project to move.

He did not defend himself. He listened — and what I remember most clearly is what came next: he asked, step by step, how to approach the shift in practice. How to define the stopping criterion before beginning. How to distinguish "thorough enough" from "stopping short." How to recognize in real time when productive depth had become diminishing returns. He took notes on each point.

In the months that followed, he began checking in early in new projects — not on outputs, but on scoping: Is the objective precise enough? Are the stopping points clear? Those check-ins were short and frequent at first. Over time they grew less frequent, not because he had stopped caring about rigor, but because he had internalized the discipline well enough to apply it without external anchoring. The project that followed that conversation delivered on schedule — which is not incidental, because scoping discipline is a precondition for timeline integrity, and he now brings it into the room before anyone thinks to ask.

Most people take feedback and apply it once. Nguyen applied it until it became instinct — and then he started asking for more.
